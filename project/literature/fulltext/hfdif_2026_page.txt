Abstract

Feature representation diversity serves as a fundamental driver for improving the effectiveness and generalization of unsupervised anomaly detection. In particular, deep random projections, as employed in Deep Isolation Forest (DIF), offer greater partitioning flexibility and enable nonlinear partitioning over subspaces of varying sizes. However, the optimization-free randomness of such projections also injects redundancy, instability, and representational ambiguity that erode representation quality and robustness, thereby constraining detection performance. To address this limitation, this paper proposes the hierarchical feature-selected deep isolation forest (HF-DIF). HF-DIF refines diverse deep random projections via a hierarchical feature-selection scheme, which promotes informative feature diversity while alleviating superfluous features, inconsistency, and representational uncertainty. Specifically, the hierarchical feature-selection scheme is driven by an iForest-based feature evaluator that quantifies feature distinguishability using forest-level aggregates of isolation-tree characteristics, including branch-imbalance and path-length statistics, thereby enabling both local and global refinement of projected representations. Theoretical analysis establishes that HF-DIF has stronger capability than DIF in separating anomalous samples from normal ones. Extensive experiments on 20 benchmark tabular datasets show that HF-DIF consistently outperforms the strongest competing baselines, improving dataset-averaged AUC-ROC by 6.2 percentage points, AUC-PR by 3.5 percentage points, and Precision@K by 5.1 percentage points. Two-sided paired \(t\)-tests confirm that HF-DIF significantly outperforms all competing baselines on AUC-ROC and Precision@K, while its AUC-PR advantage remains statistically significant over most baselines. HF-DIF advances unsupervised anomaly detection on tabular data by simultaneously preserving feature diversity and reinforcing discriminability.

1 Introduction

Anomaly detection, also known as outlier detection, is referred to as the process of detecting data points that significantly deviate from the majority of data points (Pang et al. 2021). In recent years, anomaly detection has emerged as a pivotal task across diverse academic and industrial fields, such as industrial equipment monitoring (Zhang et al. 2025a), cybersecurity (Ji et al. 2025), bearing fault diagnosis (Ma et al. 2025), business process management (Guan et al. 2025), vehicle maintenance (Ho et al. 2025).

Based on the level of availability and usability, anomaly detection can be categorized as supervised anomaly detection, semi-supervised anomaly detection, unsupervised anomaly detection, self-supervised anomaly detection, and weakly supervised anomaly detection (Pang et al. 2021). Note that unsupervised anomaly detection methods do not require any labels, they can adapt to the underlying structure of data without being biased by pre-labeled example (He et al. 2023). This allows them to detect a wide range of anomalies that might not have been seen during the training process of supervised methods. This paper focuses on unsupervised anomaly detection that is more commonly-used in practice.

Unsupervised anomaly detection methods can be roughly categorized as distance-based methods (Ahmed et al. 2022), clustering based methods (Liu et al. 2024), statistical algorithms (Wang et al. 2023), subspace-based methods (Guo et al. 2024), one-class classifiers (Qiao et al. 2023), distribution estimating methods (Li et al. 2023), isolation-based methods (Cao et al. 2024). Compared to other types of unsupervised anomaly detection methods, isolation-based unsupervised methods have several advantages (Cao et al. 2024), such as linear time complexity, small memory requirement, unsupervised learning, robustness to noise, irrelevant attributes, and data density distribution, making them a valuable tool for both researchers and practitioners. The main idea of isolation-based methods is to employ an isolation mechanism to construct isolating partitions in the input data space, such that anomalies are more likely to be isolated from the rest of the data. The most representative isolation-based method is isolation forest (iForest) as well as its variants (Cao et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2023a).

iForest (Liu et al. 2012) employs the linear axis-parallel isolation that makes the branch cut be either horizontal or vertical. To enhance the scope of linear cuts, the extended isolation forest (EIF) (Hariri et al. 2021) conducts isolation using hyperplanes with random slopes which leads to the incorporation of multiple dimensions and the improvement of robustness. However, both iForst and EIF still adhere to linear partitioning. This limitation makes these isolation-based methods encounter challenges on objects with complex distributions where anomalies cannot be isolated using pure linear partitions on single features or basic combinations of multiple features.

Therefore, a natural idea for the limitation of linear partitioning is to liberate the isolation methods from the underlying linear constraints ((Pang et al. 2021; Xu et al. 2023a)). The feature representation capability of deep forest architectures has attracted growing attention in both unsupervised and supervised learning domains (Zhang et al. 2025b). Recently, Xu et al. (Xu et al. 2023a) propose a high-level generalization of iForest, named Deep Isolation Forest (DIF). The main novelty of DIF is to leverage the powerful representation capability of neural networks to project raw data objects into an ensemble of diverse feature spaces. Each feature in the newly projected feature space is based on nonlinear interactions among a number of original features, which means that axis-parallel partitions of new space can form effective nonlinear cuts in the original feature space.

To illustrate the effect of random representation on anomaly detection, this paper employs the toy example (used in Xu et al. 2023a) as shown in Fig. 1. Figure 1(a) shows the original synthetic dataset where anomalies (red triangles) are surrounded by normal objects (blue dots), which makes anomalies cannot be isolated by either x-axis or y-axis slicing cuts. Even if these anomalies would ultimately be isolated via multiple partitions, the isolation depths achieved for anomalies by isolation trees (iTrees) are usually indistinguishable from those of normal objects. This problem constitutes a bottleneck that impedes the advancement of iForest and its variants. Figure 1(b), Fig. 1(c) and Fig. 1(d) show the random representations of projected data objects by three randomly initialized neural networks in DIF, respectively. Figure 1(b) illustrates that although projected anomalies and normal objects are more widely dispersed than in the original distributions, projected anomalies still remain surrounded by normal objects. Figure 1(c) illustrates that while projected anomalies and normal objects generally appear in distinct regions, the projected anomalies are closely distributed around the concave-shaped distribution of projected normal objects. Figure 1(d) shows that the majority of projected anomalies are distributed in regions where they are easily separable from projected normal objects.

Xu et al. (Xu et al. 2023a) prove that the random representation ensemble of DIF indeed enhances the separability of anomalies. However, there is no guarantee that projected features are definitely suitable for anomaly detection tasks. The randomness may negatively affect the stability of the models, which leads to large variance in results (Pecher et al. 2023). The promotion effect of DIF varies significantly, which is largely owing to the randomness in the one-time building process (i.e., just random initialization without optimization) of neural networks in DIF. Some projected features inevitably involve overlapped, inconsistency or even confusion of representation for anomaly detection, which is mainly caused by the randomness and the optimization-free property of the multilayer neural networks employed in DIF.

Therefore, an efficient way is required to not only explore the diversity but also restrain the instability of random representation ensemble for anomaly detection. If an effective method can be developed to select the features shown in Fig. 1(d) from those in Fig. 1(b)—Fig. 1(d) as the input to the anomaly detector, the anomaly-detection performance can be greatly improved. For exploring feature diversity, the random representation ensemble of DIF has demonstrated sufficient potential. To mitigate the representation instability, three commonly employed solutions are predominantly utilized to minimize the impact stemming from randomness (Pecher et al. 2023), i.e., regularization, ensemble, and feature selection. Regularization techniques usually impose a penalty on model’s complexity or smoothness so as to improve stability and generalization performance. Regularization techniques usually play a role in the model optimization process. Empirical evidences (Brandão Junior et al. 2022) suggest that the regularization techniques can significantly enhance the robustness of machine learning models against perturbations. However, DIF (Xu et al. 2023a) is an optimization-free model which does not considers regularization techniques.

The general workflow of ensemble methods is to create a set of base models, either by using different algorithms or by using the same algorithm with different parameters or subsets of data (Lucas et al. 2023). Then the ensemble methods usually apply some strategies to aggregate the outputs of the base models. The final output is the result ideally represents the consensus of the base models (Lucas et al. 2023).

Beyond traditional aggregation, deploying diverse and complementary machine learning models has proven highly valuable for capturing complex nonlinear relationships. Recent advancements demonstrate this trend across multiple paradigms, including neural networks (Jin et al. 2025; Jin and Xu 2025a, 2025b), Gaussian process regression (Jin and Xu 2024a, 2024b, 2025c), graphical techniques (Jin and Xu 2025d, 2025e; Xu and Zhang 2023a), and composite ensemble methods (Xu 2020, 2017; Xu and Zhang 2021). Exploring these alternatives is timely and consistently yields substantive gains in modeling complicated patterns. Motivated by the success of such diverse structures, DIF (Xu et al. 2023a) usually maintains a relatively large scale of the random representation ensemble, which utilizes the ensemble method to reduce instability. However, the large-scale representation ensemble leads to high structural complexity and heavy inference computational complexity.

The redundant features increase computational cost and degrade the performance of anomaly detection methods (Qiao et al. 2023). The focus of feature selection is to choose a subset of features from the input which can efficiently describe the input data while reducing effects from noise or irrelevant features and still provide good performance (Theng and Bhoyar 2024). Feature selection techniques are essential for creating reliable and robust machine learning models (Lin et al. 2022). DIF employs iForests to deal with the random representation members (subspaces). Thus, if an efficient feature selection method can be developed leveraging the intrinsic properties of iForest, it would improve performance stability and facilitate effective integration with DIF.

Recently, Carletti et al. (Carletti et al. 2023) propose a feature evaluation method based on the inner structure of iForest, which provides a reliable way to select specific features that tend to amplify the distinctiveness of anomalies. Carletti et al.’s method (Carletti et al. 2023) also provides significant inspiration for the efficient feature selection in DIF.

To achieve not only the inheritance of representation diversity but also the effective enhancement of performance and stability of DIF, this paper proposes a novel deep structural anomaly detection method called the hierarchical feature-selected deep isolation forest (HF-DIF). The proposed HF-DIF introduces a hierarchical feature selection scheme based on iForests into the random representation ensemble of DIF, which helps to select the projected features that exhibit diversity and improve distinguishability between anomalies and normal data objects. Specifically, the hierarchical feature selection scheme depends upon a simplified iForest-based feature evaluator and seamlessly integrates with the random representation ensemble of DIF. HF-DIF first implements the preliminary feature selection on the features generated within each representation member of DIF, and then selects the more distinguishable features across different representation members (subspaces) in the random representation ensemble. Furthermore, in comparison with DIF, the hierarchical feature selection scheme does not introduce excessive complexity, since features are evaluated and selected based on the existing parameters of trained iForests.

The contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

An iForest-based feature evaluator coupled with a representation-adaptive shrinkage strategy is developed to quantify the contribution of projected features to anomaly–normal separability. Instead of performing computationally intensive optimization over projected feature sets, the proposed design structurally exploits intrinsic tree-topological statistics encoded in trained iForests, enabling anomaly-discriminative feature importance evaluation from a compact summary of projected representations instead of data-sized feature sets. The resulting feature importance estimates are subsequently reused to adaptively determine the retained dimension of each projected representation member, preserving dimensions with greater contributions to anomaly–normal separability and filtering out low-contribution dimensions. This data-dependent dimension shrinkage incurs only linear-time overhead in the projected dimension.

A hierarchical feature-selection scheme is introduced to address cross-subspace heterogeneity induced by deep random projections and to identify projected features that strengthen anomaly–normal separability for unsupervised anomaly detection. The proposed scheme adopts a two-level structure in which anomaly-discriminative features are first selected within each projected representation member and then globally refined, such that features from subspaces with stronger contributions to anomaly-normal separability are preferentially retained instead of being selected under a uniform cross-subspace allocation, whereas the effect of features from less suitable subspaces is suppressed. This hierarchical design reduces representational redundancy and ambiguity while preserving useful feature diversity, thereby ensuring that projection-induced diversity is effectively translated into stronger separability and improved anomaly ranking performance.

Although unsupervised anomaly detection has been extensively studied across various data modalities such as images, sequences, and graphs, a substantial portion of real-world applications and benchmark evaluations still rely on tabular data, where each instance is represented by a structured set of features rather than spatial or temporal patterns (Huang et al. 2025). Tabular data are widely used in industrial monitoring, financial risk assessment, and network intrusion detection, making them a cornerstone of practical anomaly detection research. In recent years, interest in unsupervised tabular anomaly detection has grown rapidly, yet existing approaches often depend on empirically designed feature transformations (Bouman et al. 2024), lacking a consistent theoretical basis for representation refinement. Moreover, the absence of explicit spatial or sequential priors renders detection performance highly sensitive to the diversity and discriminability of learned feature representations. These observations motivate the present work to focus on tabular data, aiming to develop a principled, optimization-free model that enhances representation quality and improves anomaly separability within the isolation-based learning paradigm.

The remainder of the paper is organized in the following manner: Section 2 offers an overview of related work. Section 3 reviews iForest and DIF which form the theoretical foundation of this paper. Section 4 details the methodology of the proposed HF-DIF. Section 5 provides theoretical analyses on HF-DIF. Section 6 conducts extensive experiments to verify the superiority of the proposed method. Section 7 draws the conclusion and discusses the future work.

2 Related works
2.1 IForest and its variants

iForest is a simple and effective isolation-based unsupervised anomaly detection method (Liu et al. 2012), it detects anomalies using the isolation difficulty in the data space. iForest leverages the principle that anomalies are more likely to be isolated from others. However, iForest employs the axis-parallel splitting of data space, which can cause a specific limitation, i.e., it obscures anomalies that align with the axes of normal data clusters (Cao et al. 2024). Thus, numerous variations have been proposed to address this challenge. For example, SCiForest (Liu et al. 2010) proposes a novel non-axis-parallel branching criterion that uses an optimal slicing hyper-plane for splitting instead of selecting a single feature. EIF (Hariri et al. 2021) uses the hyper-planes with random slopes and intercepts for splitting. GIF (Lesouple et al. 2021) further fixes the empty branching problem of EIF by choosing splitting thresholds from the range of projected values onto the slope direction. PID (Gopalan et al. 2019) selectively chooses dimensions with greater variance and determines split points based on the sparsity of partition branches. These extensions normally achieve better performance by introducing non-axis-parallel and/or heuristic partition. However, the major problem is that they still rely on linear isolation operations, which means it is also hard to handle complicated data that require nonlinear partitions as the isolation method. DIF (Xu et al. 2023a) has been recently proposed to incorporate isolation forest with deep random feature representations, extending iForest to complex data types. DIF significantly outperforms iForest and its existing variants not only on tabular data but also on graph and time-series data (Xu et al. 2023a).

2.2 Deep anomaly detection

Deep anomaly detection methods aim at learning feature representations via neural networks for the sake of anomaly detection (Pang et al. 2021). Deep learning methods facilitate end-to-end optimization of the entire anomaly detection pipeline and enable the generation of specialized representations optimized for anomaly detection tasks. Deep anomaly detection methods are roughly classified into three categories (Huang et al. 2025):

(1)

Deep Learning for Feature Extraction. The methods of this category aim at leveraging deep learning to extract low-dimensional feature representations from high-dimensional and/or non-linearly separable data for downstream anomaly detection (Sun et al. 2024). Such methods typically rely on the assumption that the learned feature representations retain sufficient discriminative information to separate anomalies from normal instances. This assumption is often too restrictive in complex data scenarios.

(2)

Learning Feature Representations of Normality. The methods in this category learn data representations by optimizing a generic feature-learning objective that is not primarily designed for anomaly detection, such as generative modeling (Xie et al. 2022) or self-supervised classification (Jézéquel et al. 2023). Since this objective is not oriented toward anomaly detection, the resulting representations mainly summarize underlying regularities rather than being optimized to capture irregularities.

(3)

End-to-end Anomaly Score Learning. The methods in this category aim to learn scalar anomaly scores in an end-to-end manner, typically relying on some form of auxiliary supervision, such as labeled anomalies (Pang et al. 2020). However, this reliance is often impractical in applications where such supervision is unavailable.

Although DIF (Xu et al. 2023a) combines the advantages of deep learning and iForest, there remains insufficient assurance that the projected representations effectively amplify the distinctiveness between anomalies and normal data. Thus, this paper focuses on proposing an effective solution for finding distinguishable features for isolation-based anomaly detection.

2.3 Unsupervised feature selection

In contrast to supervised feature selection, which leverages class labels to quantify feature relevance via correlation or discriminative capability, unsupervised feature selection (UFS) operates without ground truth. Instead, it exploits intrinsic data characteristics—such as manifold topology, spectral properties, or reconstruction objectives—to filter out noisy or irrelevant dimensions. To identify informative dimensions in the absence of supervision, spectral and manifold-learning based approaches have been widely explored. For instance, Laplacian Score (He et al. 2005) evaluates feature relevance by measuring locality preservation on a nearest-neighbor graph, explicitly penalizing features that violate the local geometric structure of the data. Generalizing this perspective, the SPEC framework (Zhao and Liu 2007) unifies supervised and unsupervised criteria by analyzing the consistency between features and the eigen-structure of the instance similarity graph, providing a principled bridge between spectral clustering and feature relevance.

However, by evaluating features in isolation, these univariate methods do not explicitly account for feature redundancy and interactions, which are often critical for characterizing complex data structures. To address this issue, MCFS (Cai et al. 2010) couples spectral embedding with \({{\ell}}_{1}\)-regularized regression to identify a subset of features that jointly preserve multi-cluster structures, thereby capturing correlations that independent scoring might overlook. Similarly, UDFS (Yang et al. 2011) incorporates local discriminative analysis and \({{\ell}}_{\text{2,1}}\)-norm minimization into a joint framework, effectively selecting a compact feature subset that maintains discriminative information.

Despite their success in clustering, these general UFS criteria typically prioritize the preservation of the "central mass" or dominant modes of the distribution, potentially suppressing the rare, localized deviations characteristic of anomalies. To address this limitation, research in unsupervised anomaly detection has focused on identifying specific subspaces or structural components where outliers exhibit high separability. Subspace learning approaches like Feature Bagging (Lazarevic and Kumar 2005) mitigate the curse of dimensionality by aggregating outlier scores from multiple randomly sampled feature subsets, leveraging ensemble diversity to reveal anomalies. Distinct from randomized sampling, HiCS (Keller et al. 2012) targets high-contrast subspaces, explicitly identifying projections where the conditional dependence of attributes exposes outliers that are otherwise hidden in the marginal distributions of the full space. From a structural recovery standpoint, Robust PCA (Candès et al. 2011) isolates anomalies by decomposing the data matrix into a low-rank component (capturing the normal background structure) and a sparse component (capturing the deviations). More recent embedded frameworks, notably ODEFS (Cheng et al. 2020), unify feature selection with outlier scoring. By utilizing thresholded self-paced learning and a pairwise ranking loss, ODEFS iteratively optimizes feature weights to maximize anomaly separability while filtering unreliable candidates.

Despite these advances, several challenges remain central. Current selection criteria are often insufficiently tail-sensitive; objectives used in unsupervised feature selection, which typically emphasize geometry or cluster structure in high-density regions, can be weakly aligned with separability in the extreme region.

3 Preliminaries

The isolation-based anomaly detection methods (Cao et al. 2024) generally train anomaly score calculation models with training dataset, and then calculate anomaly scores for testing data objects using the trained models. The anomaly objects can be selected based on the ranking of anomaly scores with a threshold. The iForest (Liu et al. 2012) and the deep isolation forest (DIF) (Xu et al. 2023a) form the foundation of the proposed HF-DIF and they are summarized as follows.

3.1 Isolation forest (iForest)

Given the training data \(\mathcal{U}={\left[{{\varvec{u}}}_{1},{{\varvec{u}}}_{2},\dots ,{{\varvec{u}}}_{N}\right]}^{T}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{N\times d}\) where \({{\varvec{u}}}_{i}\in {R}^{d}\) denotes the \(i\)-th training data object, \(i=1,\dots ,N\). Let \(\mathcal{F}\) denote the iForest that consists of \(K\) iTrees, i.e., \(\mathcal{F}=\left\{{\tau }_{1},{\tau }_{2},\dots ,{\tau }_{K}\right\}\), where iTree \({\tau }_{k}\) is essentially a binary tree, \(k=1,\dots ,K\). Let \(z\) be the node index of iTree \({\tau }_{k}\), the first node (i.e., \(z=1\)) of \({\tau }_{k}\) corresponds to the root node. The \(z\)-th node of iTree \({\tau }_{k}\) is associated with a data pool (denoted by \({\mathcal{P}}_{\left.z\right|k}\)), a feature index of input data (denoted by \({j}_{z\left|k\right.}\)), and a split value of the feature (denoted by \({v}_{z\left|k\right.}\)).

In the training stage, the iForest algorithm (Liu et al. 2012) randomly selects \(\psi \left(\ll N\right)\) objects from \(\mathcal{U}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{N\times d}\) without replacement to form the subset \({\mathcal{S}}_{k}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{\psi \times d}\) which is used for training iTree \({\tau }_{k}\). iTree \({\tau }_{k}\) is initialized by its root node with the data pool \({\mathcal{P}}_{\left.1\right|k}={\mathcal{S}}_{k}\), where \({\mathcal{P}}_{\left.1\right|k}\) denotes the data pool of \({\tau }_{k}\)'s root node. The training process of iTree is based on the branching process of every internal node.

The branching process of the \(z\)-th node (\(z\ge 1\)) in iTree \({\tau }_{k}\) can be summarized as follows. First, randomly select the feature index \({j}_{z\left|k\right.}\) from \(\left\{\text{1,2},\dots ,d\right\}\). Second, randomly select the split value \({v}_{z\left|k\right.}\) within the minimum and maximum values of the \({j}_{z\left|k\right.}\)-th feature of \({\mathcal{P}}_{\left.z\right|k}\). Third, for every object \({\varvec{u}}\in {\mathcal{P}}_{\left.z\right|k}\), if its \({j}_{z\left|k\right.}\)-th feature value is smaller than \({v}_{z\left|k\right.}\), then the object is sent to the left branch of the \(z\)-th node, otherwise it is sent to the right branch of the \(z\)-th node. In this manner, the data pool \({\mathcal{P}}_{\left.z\right|k}\) is split into two disjoint subsets associated with the left child node and the right child node of the \(z\)-th node, respectively.

The branching process is performed recursively on the left child node and the right child node until a single data object is isolated or a predetermined depth limit is reached. Then, the branching process is performed on other internal nodes until the building of iTree \({\tau }_{k}\) is completed. The training process of iForest restarts the branching process of nodes with a new random sub-sample dataset \({\mathcal{S}}_{k+1}\) to build another iTree \({\tau }_{k+1}\). After building an ensemble of \(K\) iTrees, the training of iForest is completed. In the scoring process, each testing object \({\varvec{u}}\boldsymbol{^{\prime}}\) is passed through all built iTrees of iForest and the final anomaly score of \({\varvec{u}}\boldsymbol{^{\prime}}\) is calculated based on the expectation of the depths that \({\varvec{u}}\boldsymbol{^{\prime}}\) reached in all iTrees (Liu et al. 2012).

3.2 Deep Isolation Forest (DIF)

DIF (Xu et al. 2023a) has three essential modules: the random representation ensemble (i.e., the deep structure random projection scheme), the iForest ensemble, and the deviation-enhanced anomaly scoring function. The random representation ensemble of DIF consists of \(\Gamma\) representation members (subspaces) that are generated by the optimization-free multilayer neural networks. Let \({{\varvec{W}}}_{0}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{d\times m}\) denote the base weight matrix of a neural network layer, \({{\varvec{p}}}_{r}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{d}\) and \({{\varvec{q}}}_{r}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{m}\) denote the \(d\)-dimensional and the \(m\)-dimensional rank-one random vectors, respectively, \(r=\text{1,2},\dots ,\Gamma .\) DIF produces the weight matrices for the members in the random representation ensemble by

$$W_r=w_{0^\circ\left(p_rq_r^T\right)}$$	(1)

where \(\circ\) denotes the Hadamard product, \(r=\text{1,2},\dots ,\Gamma\).

Then, the training data set \(\mathcal{U}\) is projected by \(\Gamma\) weight matrices, \({{\varvec{W}}}_{1},{{\varvec{W}}}_{2},\dots ,{{\varvec{W}}}_{\Gamma }\), to produce \(\Gamma\) representation members (subspaces) of the random representation ensemble. Each representation member of the random representation ensemble is calculated by

$$x_r=uw_r=\left(\left(u\circ P_r\right)w_0\right)\circ Q_r$$	(2)

where \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{N\times m}\), \(N\) is the number of data objects, \(m\) is the dimension of projected data, \(r=\text{1,2},\dots ,\Gamma\), each row in \({{\varvec{P}}}_{r}\) and \({{\varvec{Q}}}_{r}\) is duplicated \({{\varvec{p}}}_{r}\) and \({{\varvec{q}}}_{r}\), respectively. The formulaic representations are \({{\varvec{P}}}_{r}={1}_{N}{{\varvec{p}}}_{r}^{T}\) and \({{\varvec{Q}}}_{r}={1}_{N}{{\varvec{q}}}_{r}^{T}\), where \({1}_{N}\) denotes an all-one vector with size \(N\). Then the random representation ensemble can be denoted by \(\mathcal{X}=\left[{\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{X}}_{2},\dots ,{\mathcal{X}}_{\Gamma }\right]\in {\mathbb{R}}^{N\times \left(\Gamma \times m\right)}\).

In the training process, DIF assigns each \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{N\times m}\) (see (2)) to its corresponding iForest \({\mathcal{F}}_{r}\) with \(K\) iTrees. The iForest \({\mathcal{F}}_{r}\) is denoted as \({\mathcal{F}}_{r}=\left\{{\tau }_{1,r},{\tau }_{2,r},\dots ,{\tau }_{K,r}\right\}\) where \({\tau }_{k,r}\) denotes the k-th iTree of \({\mathcal{F}}_{r}\), \(k=\text{1,2},\dots ,K\), and \(r=\text{1,2},\dots ,\Gamma\). Furthermore, the iForest ensemble of DIF is denoted by \(\mathcal{F}=\left\{{\mathcal{F}}_{1},{\mathcal{F}}_{2},\dots ,{\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma }\right\}={\left\{{\tau }_{k,r}\right\}}_{K\times\Gamma }\). DIF selects \(K\) subsets from each \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\) without replacement. \(\psi\) data objects are selected from each subset for training an iTree in \({\mathcal{F}}_{r}\). Let \({\mathcal{S}}_{k,r}\) denote the \(k\)-th subset selected from \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\), where \(\left|{\mathcal{S}}_{k,r}\right|=\psi ,k=\text{1,2},\dots ,K,\) and \(r=\text{1,2},\dots ,\Gamma\). Each iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\) is initialized from its root node with the data pool \({\mathcal{P}}_{\left.1\right|k,r}={\mathcal{S}}_{k,r}\), where \({\mathcal{P}}_{\left.1\right|k,r}\) denotes the data pool for the root node of iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\) and it corresponds to the subset \({\mathcal{S}}_{k,r}\). The architecture of the random representation ensemble and the iForest ensemble of DIF are illustrated in Fig. 2.

$${\mathcal{P}}_{2\text{z}\left|\text{k},\text{r}\right.}=\left\{\text{x}|{\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}\left|\text{k},\text{r}\right.}\right)}\le {\text{v}}_{\text{z}\left|\text{k},\text{r}\right.},\text{x}\in {\mathcal{P}}_{\text{z}\left|\text{k},\text{r}\right.}\right\}$$	(3)
$${\mathcal{P}}_{2z+1\left|k,r\right.}=\left\{x|{x}^{\left({j}_{z\left|k,r\right.}\right)}>{v}_{z\left|k,r\right.},x\in {\mathcal{P}}_{z\left|k,r\right.}\right\}$$	(4)

where \({\mathcal{P}}_{z\left|k,r\right.}={\mathcal{P}}_{2z\left|k,r\right.}\cup {\mathcal{P}}_{2z+1\left|k,r\right.}\), \({\mathcal{P}}_{2z\left|k,r\right.}\cap {\mathcal{P}}_{2z+1\left|k,r\right.}=\phi\), \({j}_{z\left|k,r\right.}\) denotes the feature index corresponding to the \(z\)-th node of iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\), \({j}_{z\left|k,r\right.}\) is randomly sampled from the feature index set of the projected data space, i.e., \(\left\{\text{1,2},\dots ,m\right\}\), \({{\varvec{x}}}^{\left({j}_{z\left|k\right.,r}\right)}\) denotes the \({j}_{z\left|k,r\right.}\)-th feature (dimension) of \({\varvec{x}}\in {\mathcal{P}}_{z\left|k,r\right.}\), and \({v}_{z\left|k,r\right.}\) denotes the splitting threshold value for the z-th node of iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\), \({v}_{z\left|k,r\right.}\) is randomly selected within the range of \(\left[min\left({{\varvec{x}}}^{\left({j}_{z\left|k,r\right.}\right)}\right),max\left({{\varvec{x}}}^{\left({j}_{z\left|k,r\right.}\right)}\right)\right]\). The branching process (refer to (3) and (4)) is performed recursively on the left child node (the 2 \(z\)-th node) and the right child node (the (\(2z+1\))-th node) until a single data object is isolated or a predetermined depth limit is reached. The termination node of iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\) has no associated feature index.

After the construction of all iForests \({\mathcal{F}}_{r}\in \mathcal{F}\), \(r=\text{1,2},\dots ,\Gamma\), the abnormality of a data object is ready for evaluation based on the isolation difficulty of each iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\) in \(\mathcal{F}\). To enhance the measurement of isolation difficulty, DIF incorporates the deviation degree between the feature value and the split value as supplementary weighting information. Let \(\mathcal{Z}\left({\varvec{x}}|{\tau }_{k,r}\right)\) denote the traverse node set of \({\varvec{x}}\in {\mathcal{X}}_{r}\) over iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\) (i.e., the set of nodes traversed by \({\varvec{x}}\) from the root node to the termination node of iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\)), the averaged deviation degree of \({\varvec{x}}\) in iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\) is

$$g\left({\varvec{x}}|{\tau }_{k,r}\right)=\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{Z}\left({\varvec{x}}|{\tau }_{k,r}\right)\right|}\sum_{z\in \mathcal{Z}\left({\varvec{x}}|{\tau }_{k,r}\right)}\left|{{\varvec{x}}}^{\left({j}_{z\left|k,r\right.}\right)}-{v}_{z\left|k,r\right.}\right|$$	(5)

where \(\left|\bullet \right|\) denotes the operator for calculating the set size.

Then the deviation-enhanced anomaly scoring function (the third essential module) of DIF for the object \({\varvec{u}}\) can be defined by

$$s\left({\varvec{u}}|\mathcal{F}\right)={2}^{-{\mathbb{E}}_{{{\varvec{x}}\in {\mathcal{X}}_{r},\tau }_{k,r}\in \mathcal{F}}\left[\left|\mathcal{Z}\left({\varvec{x}}|{\tau }_{k,r}\right)\right|\right]/c\left(n\right)}\times {\mathbb{E}}_{{\varvec{x}}\in {\mathcal{X}}_{r},{\tau }_{k,r}\in \mathcal{F}}\left[g\left({\varvec{x}}|{\tau }_{k,r}\right)\right]$$	(6)

Where c(n) denotes the normalizing factor (Xu et al. 2023a), n denotes the size of data for constructing iTree.

4 The proposed method

In this section, the proposed HF-DIF is first overviewed in Section 4.1. Section 4.2 provides the operations of data projection and iForest training in HF-DIF. Section 4.3 explicates the key module of HF-DIF, i.e., iForest-based Feature Evaluator (iFFE), step-by-step. Section 4.4 illustrates the hierarchical feature selection based on iFFE. Section 4.5 describes the testing stage of HF-DIF with the trained iForest, the initialized network weights and the testing data. Section 4.6 provides the formulation and implementation of the representation-adaptive shrinkage strategy. Finally, the pseudo-code algorithms for HF-DIF are provided in Section 4.7.

4.1 Framework overview

The proposed HF-DIF inherits most structure of DIF, the novelty of HF-DIF is the hierarchical feature selection scheme that consists of both the internal (first-level) feature selection and the cross (second-level) feature selection. It makes the distinguishable features be selected from both local perspective and global perspective.

In the training process, HF-DIF projects the training data objects to new feature spaces (representation members) via randomly initialized neural networks; HF-DIF evaluates features of each projected feature space and obtains their importance scores using the iForest-based feature evaluation method; HF-DIF sorts the importance scores of features in each projected feature space and then stores the corresponding feature indices into a list. The list of feature indices provides the candidates for the first-level feature selection within each projected feature space. HF-DIF concatenates these selected features from different projected feature spaces, and then it implements the second-level feature evaluation to capture the most distinguishable features across different projected features. HF-DIF trains the iForest based on the hierarchically selected features, and retains indices of the finally selected features for the testing stage.

The training pipeline of HF-DIF is shown in Fig. 3. In the left panel of Fig. 3, training data \(\mathcal{U}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{N\times d}\) are projected using randomly initialized neural networks (their parameters denoted by \({{\varvec{W}}}_{1},{{\varvec{W}}}_{2},\dots ,{{\varvec{W}}}_{\Gamma }\)) to generate projected data \({\mathcal{X}}_{1},\dots ,{\mathcal{X}}_{\Gamma }\); subsequently, the features in each projected feature space are evaluated by the corresponding iFFE to produce their importance scores. In the first-level feature selection, features of each \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\) with high importance scores are selected, the corresponding indices of these selected features are stored in the list denoted by \({\widehat{{\varvec{\Omega}}}}_{r},r=\text{1,2},\dots ,\Gamma\). Upon identifying the feature indices in \({\widehat{{\varvec{\Omega}}}}_{r}\), the feature selection step (represented by the FS block in Fig. 3) utilizes them to extract the corresponding feature columns from \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\), forming \({\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}_{r}\) and effectively discarding all remaining feature dimensions. Next, the feature subsets \(\left\{{\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}_{r}\right\}\) and their corresponding index lists \(\left\{{\widehat{{\varvec{\Omega}}}}_{r}\right\}\) are concatenated to yield \(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}\) and \(\widehat{{\varvec{\Omega}}}\), respectively.

As illustrated in the right panel of Fig. 3, the second-level feature selection is applied to \(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}\) in a manner analogous to the first level to generate the globally selected features \(\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}\), utilizing the iFFE mechanism in conjunction with \(\widehat{{\varvec{\Omega}}}\) to evaluate the global importance of the concatenated features. The globally selected features \(\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}\) are then used to train the iForest for anomaly detection purpose. Moreover, both \(\widehat{{\varvec{\Omega}}}\) and \(\widetilde{{\varvec{\Omega}}}\) are fed into the backtracking module (represented by the BT block in Fig. 3). The backtracking module traces the indices of the globally selected features \(\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}\) back to their corresponding dimensions in the original projected spaces \({\mathcal{X}}_{1},\dots ,{\mathcal{X}}_{\Gamma }\), thereby identifying the specific feature indices within each original space to generate the index lists \({{\varvec{\Omega}}}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{^{\prime}}},{{\varvec{\Omega}}}_{2}^{\boldsymbol{^{\prime}}},\dots ,\boldsymbol{ }{{\varvec{\Omega}}}_{\Gamma }^{\boldsymbol{^{\prime}}}\). Furthermore, \({{\varvec{\Omega}}}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{^{\prime}}},{{\varvec{\Omega}}}_{2}^{\boldsymbol{^{\prime}}},\dots ,\boldsymbol{ }{{\varvec{\Omega}}}_{\Gamma }^{\boldsymbol{^{\prime}}}\) can be sent to the testing process of HF-DIF (shown in Fig. 4) which make the input testing data be directly transformed to the hierarchically selected representation. Therefore, the backtracking of feature indices contributes to establishing a direct mapping from the hierarchical selected data set to the original projected data sets.

In the testing process, HF-DIF projects the testing data objects to new feature spaces using the same neural networks fixed in the training stage. HF-DIF implements the feature selection on the projected testing data according to the feature index lists obtained by the backtracking module in the training process. Figure 4 illustrates the testing pipeline of HF-DIF. Specifically, the testing data \({\mathcal{U}}^{\boldsymbol{^{\prime}}}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{{N}{\prime}\times d}\) are projected via the learned neural network parameters \({{\varvec{W}}}_{1},{{\varvec{W}}}_{2},\dots ,{{\varvec{W}}}_{\Gamma }\) to yield projected data \({\mathcal{X}}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{^{\prime}}},\dots ,{\mathcal{X}}_{\Gamma }^{\boldsymbol{^{\prime}}}\). Subsequently, HF-DIF performs feature selection (represented as FS block in Fig. 4) on these projected testing data \({\mathcal{X}}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{^{\prime}}},\dots ,{\mathcal{X}}_{\Gamma }^{\boldsymbol{^{\prime}}}\) using the feature index lists \({{\varvec{\Omega}}}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{^{\prime}}},{{\varvec{\Omega}}}_{2}^{\boldsymbol{^{\prime}}},\dots ,\boldsymbol{ }{{\varvec{\Omega}}}_{\Gamma }^{\boldsymbol{^{\prime}}}\) established by the backtracking module during training. Critically, by leveraging these pre-determined index lists, the feature selection during testing is streamlined into an efficient look-up table operation, eliminating the need for iterative search or complex optimization at inference time. Finally, the selected feature representations \({\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}_{1}{\prime},\dots ,{\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}_{\Gamma }{\prime}\) are concatenated into \({\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}}^{\boldsymbol{^{\prime}}}\), which serves as the input to the trained iForest to produce final anomaly scores.

4.2 Deep projection and iforest training

Similar with DIF, HF-DIF first projects the training dataset \(\mathcal{U}\) to the ensemble of new feature spaces using the randomly initialized neural networks without any optimization. The r-th projection for training data set \(\mathcal{U}\) can be obtained based on (1) and (2) as follows,

$${\mathcal{X}}_{r}=\mathcal{U}{W}_{r}=\left(\left(\mathcal{U}\circ {P}_{r}\right){W}_{0}\right){\circ Q}_{r}$$

where \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{N\times m}\) denotes the projected representation for original data \(\mathcal{U}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{N\times d}\), \(N\) denotes the number of data objects, \(m\) denotes the dimension of projected data, \(r=\text{1,2},\dots ,\Gamma\), the derivations of \({{\varvec{W}}}_{r}\), \({{\varvec{P}}}_{r}\), and \({{\varvec{Q}}}_{r}\) can be found in (1) and (2). Since the dimension of the projected is \(m\), the index set of \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\) can be denoted by \({{\varvec{\Omega}}}_{r}=\left\{\text{1,2},\dots ,m\right\}\).

Then, HF-DIF also assigns each \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{N\times m}\) (see (2)) to its corresponding iForest \({\mathcal{F}}_{r}\) for training purpose, \(r=\text{1,2},\dots ,\Gamma\). The training process of iForests \({\mathcal{F}}_{r}\) with \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\) can refer to (3) and (4). Once the training of all iForests (\(\mathcal{F}=\left\{{\mathcal{F}}_{1},{\mathcal{F}}_{2},\dots ,{\mathcal{F}}_{\Gamma }\right\}={\left\{{\tau }_{k,r}\right\}}_{K\times\Gamma }\)) is completed, HF-DIF starts executing the iForest-based Feature Evaluator module (i.e., the iFFE module shown in Fig. 3).

4.3 iForest-based Feature Evaluator (iFFE)

Inspired by Carletti et al. (2023), the evaluation of feature importances for anomaly detection mainly relies on two quantitative properties. First, the important feature generally produces higher imbalance branches for anomaly data objects, while it does not have much effect on normal ones. Second, the important feature usually guides anomaly objects to the termination nodes with small traverse lengths, while guides normal objects to the termination nodes with large traverse lengths. To take into account the above two properties while simultaneously leveraging the characteristics of DIF, this paper proposes the iForest-based feature evaluator (iFFE) as an embedded module of HF-DIF (see iFFE block in Fig. 3). The detailed operations of iFFE are as provided follows.

Step 1 Initial Partition. HF-DIF computes anomaly scores of the projected data vector \({\varvec{x}}\in {\mathcal{X}}_{r}\) based on the corresponding trained iForest \({\mathcal{F}}_{r}\). The computation formula for these anomaly scores can be obtained by replacing iForest ensemble \(\mathcal{F}\) in (6) with the trained \({\mathcal{F}}_{r}\) as

$$s\left({\varvec{x}}|{\mathcal{F}}_{r}\right)={2}^{-{\mathbb{E}}_{{{\varvec{x}}\in {\mathcal{X}}_{r},\tau }_{k,r}\in {\mathcal{F}}_{r}}\left(\left|\mathcal{Z}\left({\varvec{x}}|{\tau }_{k,r}\right)\right|/c\left(n\right)\right)}\times {\mathbb{E}}_{{\varvec{x}}\in {\mathcal{X}}_{r},{\tau }_{k,r}\in {\mathcal{F}}_{r}}\left(g\left({\varvec{x}}|{\tau }_{k,r}\right)\right)$$	(7)

where \({\mathcal{F}}_{r}=\left\{{\tau }_{1,r},{\tau }_{2,r},\dots ,{\tau }_{K,r}\right\}\) denotes the iForest corresponding to \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\) that involves \(K\) iTrees, \(\mathcal{Z}\left({\varvec{x}}|{\tau }_{k,r}\right)\) denotes the traverse node set of \({\varvec{x}}\in {\mathcal{X}}_{r}\) over iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\) (i.e., the set of nodes traversed by \({\varvec{x}}\) from the root node to the termination node of iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\)), \(g\left({\varvec{x}}|{\tau }_{k,r}\right)\) is defined by (5), \(k=\text{1,2},\dots ,K\), and \(r=\text{1,2},\dots ,\Gamma\).

HF-DIF sorts the anomaly scores of all data \({\varvec{x}}\in {\mathcal{X}}_{r}\) in descending order. Based on the ranking of anomaly scores and the anomaly score threshold \(\theta \left(>0\right)\), HF-DIF partitions the projected data set \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\) into the anomaly data subset \({\mathcal{A}}_{r}\) and the normal data subset \({\mathcal{N}}_{r}\). \({\mathcal{A}}_{r}\) and \({\mathcal{N}}_{r}\) satisfy \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}={\mathcal{A}}_{r}\bigcup {\mathcal{N}}_{r}\) and \({\mathcal{A}}_{r}\cap {\mathcal{N}}_{r}=\boldsymbol{\varnothing }\).

Step 2 The Computation of Imbalance Branch Coefficients. Suppose the \(z\)-th node in iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\) have child nodes (i.e., the \(z\)-th node is the root node or the internal node of iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\)). Then, the branch imbalance coefficient of the z-th node in iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\), i.e., \({BIC}_{z\left|k,r\right.}\), is defined by

$${BIC}_{z\left|k,r\right.}=\left\{\begin{array}{c}0, if \left|{\mathcal{P}}_{z\left|k,r\right.}\right|=0\\ \frac{max\left(\left|{\mathcal{P}}_{2z\left|k,r\right.}\right|,\left|{\mathcal{P}}_{2z+1\left|k,r\right.}\right|\right)}{\left|{\mathcal{P}}_{z\left|k,r\right.}\right|}, otherwise\end{array}\right.$$	(8)

where \({\mathcal{P}}_{2z\left|k,r\right.}\), and \({\mathcal{P}}_{2z+1\left|k,r\right.}\) are given in (3) and (4), \(\left|\cdot \right|\) denotes the set size operator, \(k=\text{1,2},\dots ,K\), \(r=\text{1,2},\dots ,\Gamma\). \({BIC}_{z\left|k,r\right.}\) equals 0 when the data pool of the z-th node in iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\) is empty; otherwise, \({BIC}_{z\left|k,r\right.}\) represents the imbalance degree caused by the feature contained in the \(z\)-th node.

During the construction process of iTrees, each internal node of iTree selects a feature from input feature space. Therefore, each internal node of an iTree corresponds to a feature. On the contrary, one feature of input data may correspond to many internal nodes of the iTree. To estimate the imbalance branch effect of a feature, it is necessary to conduct statistical analysis on the internal nodes of the iTree that correspond to the same feature. As mentioned above, the difference between anomaly data and normal data should be considered. Hence, for the anomaly data set \({\mathcal{A}}_{r}\), the average anomaly imbalance branch coefficient of the j-th feature w.r.t. iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\) is given by

$${\mathcal{B}a}_{j\left|k,r\right.}=\frac{1}{\left|{\mathcal{A}}_{r}\right|}\sum_{{\varvec{x}}\in {\mathcal{A}}_{r}}\sum_{z\in \mathcal{Z}\left({\varvec{x}}|{\tau }_{k,r}\right)}{\mathbb{I}}\left({j}_{z\left|k,r\right.}=j\right)\cdot {BIC}_{z\left|k,r\right.}$$	(9)

where \(j\) denotes the feature index of the input data, \({j}_{z\left|k,r\right.}\) denotes the feature index corresponding to the \(z\)-th node of iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\), \(j,{j}_{z\left|k,r\right.}=\text{1,2},\dots ,m\), \({BIC}_{z\left|k,r\right.}\) is given in (8).

For normal data set \({\mathcal{N}}_{r}\), the average normal imbalance branch coefficient of the j-th feature w.r.t. iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\) is given by

$${\mathcal{B}n}_{j\left|k,r\right.}=\frac{1}{\left|{\mathcal{N}}_{r}\right|}\sum_{{\varvec{x}}\in {\mathcal{N}}_{r}}\sum_{z\in \mathcal{Z}\left({\varvec{x}}|{\tau }_{k,r}\right)}{\mathbb{I}}\left({j}_{z\left|k,r\right.}=j\right)\cdot {BIC}_{z\left|k,r\right.}$$	(10)

\({\mathcal{B}a}_{j\left|k,r\right.}\) and \({\mathcal{B}n}_{j\left|k,r\right.}\) indicate the imbalance degrees estimated based on the anomaly data set \({\mathcal{A}}_{r}\) and the normal data set \({\mathcal{N}}_{r}\), respectively.

Step 3 The Computation of Average Visiting Count and Terminate depth. While computing the anomaly scores (see (7)), certain essential statistics related with feature importance evaluation can be derived concurrently without consuming too much extra computational complexity. If the z-th internal node in iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\) corresponds to the j-th feature of \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\) (i.e., \({j}_{z\left|k,r\right.}=j\)), and an object \({\varvec{x}}\in {\mathcal{X}}_{r}\) traverses internal node \(z\), then the visiting count of the j-th feature w.r.t. iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\) adds 1, and the terminate depth of the j-th feature with respect to iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\) adds \(\left|\mathcal{Z}\left({\varvec{x}}|{\tau }_{k,r}\right)\right|\), where \(\mathcal{Z}\left({\varvec{x}}|{\tau }_{k,r}\right)\) denotes the set of nodes traversed by \({\varvec{x}}\) from the root node to the termination node of iTree \({\tau }_{k,r}\).

For anomaly data set \({\mathcal{A}}_{r}\), the average visiting count and the average terminate depth corresponding to the j-th feature are defined by (11) and (12), respectively.

$${aVC}_{j\left|k,r\right.}=\frac{1}{\left|{\mathcal{A}}_{r}\right|}\sum_{{\varvec{x}}\in {\mathcal{A}}_{r}}\sum_{z\in \mathcal{Z}\left({\varvec{x}}|{\tau }_{k,r}\right)}{\mathbb{I}}\left({j}_{z\left|k,r\right.}=j\right)$$	(11)
$${aTD}_{j\left|k,r\right.}=\frac{1}{\left|{\mathcal{A}}_{r}\right|}\sum_{{\varvec{x}}\in {\mathcal{A}}_{r}}\sum_{z\in \mathcal{Z}\left({\varvec{x}}|{\tau }_{k,r}\right)}{\mathbb{I}}\left({j}_{z\left|k,r\right.}=j\right)\bullet \left|\mathcal{Z}\left({\varvec{x}}|{\tau }_{k,r}\right)\right|$$	(12)

For normal data set \({\mathcal{N}}_{r}\), the average visiting count and the average terminate depth corresponding to the j-th feature are defined by (13) and (14), respectively.

$${nVC}_{j\left|k,r\right.}=\frac{1}{\left|{\mathcal{N}}_{r}\right|}\sum_{{\varvec{x}}\in {\mathcal{N}}_{r}}\sum_{z\in \mathcal{Z}\left({\varvec{x}}|{\tau }_{k,r}\right)}{\mathbb{I}}\left({j}_{z\left|k,r\right.}=j\right)$$	(13)
$${nTD}_{j\left|k,r\right.}=\frac{1}{\left|{\mathcal{N}}_{r}\right|}\sum_{{\varvec{x}}\in {\mathcal{N}}_{r}}\sum_{z\in \mathcal{Z}\left({\varvec{x}}|{\tau }_{k,r}\right)}{\mathbb{I}}\left({j}_{z\left|k,r\right.}=j\right)\bullet \left|\mathcal{Z}\left({\varvec{x}}|{\tau }_{k,r}\right)\right|$$	(14)

If the feature contributes the detection of anomalies, its average visiting count and average terminate depth for anomaly data should be the smaller the better; simultaneously, for normal data, its average visiting count and average terminate depth should be the larger the better.

Step 4 The Computation of Feature Importance Scores. The proposed HF-DIF assigns each projected data set \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\) to the corresponding iForest \({\mathcal{F}}_{r}\), \(r=\text{1,2},\dots ,\Gamma\). The feature importance of \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\) evaluated by \({\mathcal{F}}_{r}\) should take into account the \(K\) iTrees of \({\mathcal{F}}_{r}\) together. Based on (8)-(14), the partial feature importance coefficients for anomaly data and normal data are computed by (15) and (16), respectively.

$${aFI}_{j\left|r\right.}=\frac{1}{{\sum }_{k=1}^{K}{aVC}_{j\left|k,r\right.}}\cdot \sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{{\mathcal{B}a}_{j\left|k,r\right.}}{{aTD}_{j\left|k,r\right.}}$$	(15)
$${nFI}_{j\left|r\right.}=\frac{1}{{\sum }_{k=1}^{K}{nVC}_{j\left|k,r\right.}}\cdot \sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{{\mathcal{B}n}_{j\left|k,r\right.}}{{nTD}_{j\left|k,r\right.}}$$	(16)

where \(j=\text{1,2},\dots ,m\), \({aFI}_{j\left|r\right.}\) and \({nFI}_{j\left|r\right.}\) denote the partial feature importance coefficients evaluated using \(K\) iTrees in \({\mathcal{F}}_{r}\) for the anomaly data set \({\mathcal{A}}_{r}\) and the normal data set \({\mathcal{N}}_{r}\), respectively.

For important features, their contributions to isolating anomaly data should beyond those to normal data. To reveal the feature importance from both anomaly data and normal data, the integrated feature importance score of the j-th feature of \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\) is defined by

$${{\ell}}_{j\left|r\right.}={aFI}_{j\left|r\right.}/{nFI}_{j\left|r\right.}$$	(17)

where \({{\ell}}_{j\left|r\right.}\) denotes the integrated feature importance score of the j-th feature of \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\) that is computed based on iForest \({\mathcal{F}}_{r}\), \(j=\text{1,2},\dots ,m, r=\text{1,2},\dots ,\Gamma\), the greater the value of \({{\ell}}_{j\left|r\right.}\), the more important the j-th feature of \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\) for anomaly detection.

4.4 Hierarchical feature selection

As mentioned in Section 4.2, HF-DIF generates \(\Gamma\) projected representation members (subspaces) from the original data set using \(\Gamma\) independent randomly initialized neural networks. The features of different projected data sets are independent with each other. Moreover, each projected data set is used to train its corresponding iForest for feature evaluation purpose, which means that the \(\Gamma\) iForests are independent with each other. Due to these independencies, the integrated feature importance scores (see (17)) obtained from the same projected feature space are comparable, thereby supporting the feature selection within the projected feature space. On the contrary, different deep random projections capture different aspects of the data structure, they exhibit varying degrees of informative density. The projected features from different subspaces do not contribute equally to anomaly–normal isolation (due to randomness). As a result, some subspaces provide projections under which the resulting features facilitate anomaly isolation strongly, whereas others yield features with limited isolation ability. This motivates a second-level feature selection that selects more features from subspaces with stronger contributions to anomaly isolation and fewer features from those with weaker contributions. The purpose of this second-level selection is to retain the most informative anomaly-discriminative features from the projected subspaces, and in doing so it also reduces the overall feature dimensionality.

HF-DIF adopts the hierarchical feature selection scheme to determine the most conducive features for anomaly detection. Firstly, HF-DIF implements the first-level feature selection module on the projected feature subspaces in parallel. Subsequently, the selected features are concatenated and then input into the second-level feature selection module to identify the most conducive features for anomaly detection. The detailed descriptions are given as follows.

Step 1 First-level feature selection. HF-DIF implements the first-level feature selection on the projected data sets, \({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{X}}_{2},\dots ,{\mathcal{X}}_{\Gamma }\), as follows. For the r-th projected data set \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{N\times m}\), HF-DIF sorts the integrated feature importance scores \({{\ell}}_{1\left|r\right.},{{\ell}}_{2\left|r\right.},\dots ,{{\ell}}_{m\left|r\right.}\) in descending order, and obtains the indices of the sorted scores, \({\lambda }_{1},{\lambda }_{2},\dots ,{\lambda }_{m}\in \left\{\text{1,2},\dots ,m\right\}\), such that \({{\ell}}_{{\lambda }_{1}\left|r\right.}>{{\ell}}_{{\lambda }_{2}\left|r\right.}>\dots >{{\ell}}_{{\lambda }_{m}\left|r\right.}\), \(r=\text{1,2},\dots ,\Gamma\). HF-DIF selects the first \(\left\lfloor\eta_rm\right\rfloor\) indices \(\lambda_1,\lambda_{2,\dots,\lambda_{\left\lfloor\eta_rm\right\rfloor}}\) to form the first-level selected index list \({\widehat\Omega}_r=\left[\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\dots,\lambda_{\left\lfloor\eta_rm\right\rfloor}\right]\), where \({\eta }_{r}\in \left[\left.1/m,1\right)\right.\) denotes the shrink coefficient for the r-th projected data set \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\), \(\left\lfloor\cdot\right\rfloor\) denotes the floor function. Since \(\left\lfloor\eta_rm\right\rfloor\) and \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\dots,\lambda_m\in\left\{1,2,\dots,m\right\}\), it can be inferred that \(\left[\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\dots,\lambda_{\left\lfloor\eta_rm\right\rfloor}\right]\) is the sub-list of \(\left[\text{1,2},\dots ,m\right]\). Hence, \({\widehat{\Omega }}_{r}\) can be considered as the sub-list of Ωr.

The elements in \({\widehat{\Omega }}_{r}\) indicate the first \(\left\lfloor\eta_rm\right\rfloor\) important features of the r-th projected data set \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{N\times m}\). The projected data set with the selected features can be denoted by \({\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}_{r}={\left({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\right)}_{{\widehat{\Omega }}_{r}}\) where \({\left(\chi_r\right)}_{{\overset\frown\Omega}_r}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times\left\lfloor\eta_rm\right\rfloor}\) denotes the submatrix of \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{N\times m}\) whose columns are selected from \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\) according to the elements of \({\widehat{\Omega }}_{r}\). Since \({\widehat{\mathcal X}}_r\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times\left\lfloor\eta_rm\right\rfloor}\), the feature (column) index list of \({\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}_{r}\) is \(\left[\text{1,2},\dots,\left\lfloor\eta_rm\right\rfloor\right]\), and the \(j\)-th feature of \({\widehat\chi}_r\) corresponds to the λj-th feature of \({\mathcal{X}}_{r}\), \({\lambda }_{j}\) denotes the j-th element in \({\widehat{\Omega }}_{r}\), \(\lambda_j\in\left\{\text{1,2},\dots,m\right\},j=\text{1,2},\dots,\left\lfloor\eta_rm\right\rfloor\), and \(r=\text{1,2},\dots ,\Gamma\). Repeat above operations for all projected data sets, \({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{X}}_{2},\dots ,{\mathcal{X}}_{\Gamma }\), to obtain the selected feature index lists \({\widehat{{\varvec{\Omega}}}}_{1},{\widehat{{\varvec{\Omega}}}}_{2},\dots ,{\widehat{{\varvec{\Omega}}}}_{\Gamma }\) and the feature-selected data sets \({\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}_{1},{\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}_{2},\dots ,{\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}_{\Gamma }\), respectively.

Step 2 Concatenation of features and feature index lists. HF-DIF concatenates \({\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}_{1},{\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}_{2},\dots ,{\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}_{\Gamma }\) along the feature dimension as \(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}=\left[{\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}_{1},{\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}_{2},\dots ,{\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}_{\Gamma }\right]\in {\mathbb{R}}^{N\times \widehat{m}}\), where \(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}\) denotes the data set that concatenates all selected features from different projected data sets in Step 1, \(\widehat m=\sum\nolimits_{r=1}^\Gamma\left\lfloor\eta_rm\right\rfloor\). The feature index list of \(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}\) is \(\left[\text{1,2},\dots ,\widehat{m}\right]\). HF-DIF concatenates feature index lists \({\widehat{{\varvec{\Omega}}}}_{1},{\widehat{{\varvec{\Omega}}}}_{2},\dots ,{\widehat{{\varvec{\Omega}}}}_{\Gamma }\) as \(\widehat{{\varvec{\Omega}}}=\left[{\widehat{{\varvec{\Omega}}}}_{1},{\widehat{{\varvec{\Omega}}}}_{2},\dots ,{\widehat{{\varvec{\Omega}}}}_{\Gamma }\right]\). Both \(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}\) and \(\widehat{{\varvec{\Omega}}}\) provide the foundation for the second-level feature selection. Step 3 Second-level feature selection. In the second-level feature selection (as shown in the right panel of Fig. 3), HF-DIF employs a new iFFE module (Section 4.3) to compute the integrated feature importance scores for all features of the concatenated data set \(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{N\times \widehat{m}}\), i.e., \({\widehat{{\ell}}}_{1},{\widehat{{\ell}}}_{2},\dots ,{\widehat{{\ell}}}_{\widehat{m}}\). The features of \(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}\) are evaluated by the shared iForest of the employed iFFE module (in the second level feature selection) which makes features selected from different projected data sets be comparable.

Then, HF-DIF sorts \({\widehat{{\ell}}}_{1},{\widehat{{\ell}}}_{2},\dots ,{\widehat{{\ell}}}_{\widehat{m}}\) in descending order and obtains the indices of the sorted scores \({\widehat{\lambda }}_{1},{\widehat{\lambda }}_{2},\dots ,{\widehat{\lambda }}_{\widehat{m}}\in \left\{\text{1,2},\dots ,\widehat{m}\right\}\) such that \({\widehat{{\ell}}}_{{\widehat{\lambda }}_{1}}>{\widehat{{\ell}}}_{{\widehat{\lambda }}_{2}}>\dots >{\widehat{{\ell}}}_{{\widehat{\lambda }}_{\widehat{m}}}\). Let \({\eta }{\prime}\in \left[\left.1/\widehat{m},1\right)\right.\) denote the shrink coefficient in the second-level feature selection, then select the first \(m'=\left\lfloor\eta'\widehat m\right\rfloor\) indices to form the second-level selected feature index list \(\widetilde{{\varvec{\Omega}}}=\left[{\widehat{\lambda }}_{1},{\widehat{\lambda }}_{2},\dots ,{\widehat{\lambda }}_{{m}{\prime}}\right]\), where \({m}{\prime}<\widehat{m}\). Correspondingly, the data representation of the second-level selected features can be generated by \(\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}={\left(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}\right)}_{\widetilde{{\varvec{\Omega}}}}\) where \({\left(\widehat{x}\right)}_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times m'}\) denotes the submatrix of \(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}\) whose columns (features) are selected from \(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}\) according to the elements of \(\widetilde{{\varvec{\Omega}}}\).

Train the second-level iForest \(\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}\) using \(\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{N\times {m}{\prime}}\), which has \({K}{\prime}\) iTrees and serves as the final detector in HF-DIF, according to the algorithm of DIF (Section 3.2) for anomaly detection purpose. It should be noted that elements of \(\widetilde{{\varvec{\Omega}}}\), i.e., \({\widehat{\lambda }}_{1},{\widehat{\lambda }}_{2},\dots ,{\widehat{\lambda }}_{{m}{\prime}}\in \left\{\text{1,2},\dots ,\widehat{m}\right\}\), are derived from the feature indices of the concatenated data set \(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}\) and do not represent the feature indices of the previously projected feature data sets \({\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}_{1},{\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}_{2},\dots ,{\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}_{\Gamma }\). This is because the feature indices of each projected feature data set \({\widehat{\mathcal{X}}}_{r}\) are distributed within the set \(\left\{\text{1,2},\dots ,m\right\}\), \(r=\text{1,2},\dots ,\Gamma\), while elements of \(\widetilde{{\varvec{\Omega}}}\) are the indices distributed within the set \(\left\{\text{1,2},\dots ,\widehat{m}\right\}\). Step 4 Backtracking of feature indices. The concatenated feature index list \(\widehat{\Omega }\) (see Step 2) is formed by \(\widehat{\Omega }=\left[{\widehat{\Omega }}_{1},{\widehat{\Omega }}_{2},\dots ,{\widehat{\Omega }}_{\Gamma }\right]\), the elements in the r-th sub-list of \(\widehat{\Omega }\) (i.e., \({\widehat{\Omega }}_{\text{r}}\)) are the indices of the features selected from the r-th projected data set \({\text{X}}_{\text{r}}\in {\text{R}}^{\text{N}\times \text{m}}\), \(\text{r}=\text{1,2},\dots ,\Gamma .\) Since the dimension of each projected data set \({\text{X}}_{\text{r}}\) is \(\text{m}\), each element in \({\widehat{\Omega }}_{\text{r}}\) is distributed within the set \(\left\{\text{1,2},\dots ,\text{m}\right\}\), \(\text{r}=\text{1,2},\dots ,\Gamma\). On the other hand, the elements of \(\widetilde{\Omega }\) denote the indices of the features in the concatenated data set \(\widehat{X}\in {R}^{N\times \widehat{m}}\). Based on the property of \(\left|{\widehat\Omega}_\text{r}\right|=\left\lfloor\eta_rm\right\rfloor\), HF-DIF first determines the index range for the sub-list \({\widehat{\Omega }}_{r}\) within \(\widehat{\Omega }\) by

$${\Delta }_{r}=\left[{\alpha }_{r-1}+1,\dots ,{\alpha }_{r-1}+\left|{\widehat{\Omega }}_{r}\right|\right]$$	(18)

where \({\Delta }_{\text{r}}\) denotes the index sub-list that delineates the index range of \({\widehat{\Omega }}_{\text{r}}\) within \(\widehat{\Omega }\), \(\text{r}=\text{1,2},\dots ,\Gamma\), and ar-1 is given by

$$\alpha_{r-1}=\left\{\begin{array}{l}0\\\begin{array}{cc}&{\textstyle\sum_{r=1}^{\Gamma-1}}\left|{\widehat\Omega}_r\right|={\textstyle\sum_{r=1}^{\Gamma-1}}\left\lfloor\eta_rm\right\rfloor\end{array}\begin{array}{c}r=2,\dots,\Gamma^{r=1}\end{array}\end{array}\right.$$	(19)

Since \(\widetilde{\Omega }\) involves the indices corresponding to the m\(\left({\eta }{\prime}\widehat{m}\right)\)’ important features of \(\widehat{\text{X}}\in {\text{R}}^{\text{N}\times \widehat{\text{m}}}\), each element of \(\widetilde{\Omega }\) is within the range of \(\left\{\text{1,2},\dots ,\widehat{m}\right\}\). HF-DIF can determine which elements of \(\widetilde{\Omega }\) are from \({\widehat{\Omega }}_{\text{r}}\) by

$$\varrho_r=\widetilde\Omega\cup\triangle_r$$	(20)

where ϱr is the sub-list of the elements (feature indices) in \(\widetilde{\Omega }\) that initially come from \({\widehat{\Omega }}_{\text{r}}\).

Then, HF-DIF converts the elements in ϱr from the element range of \({\Delta }_{\text{r}}\) to the index range of \({\widehat{\Omega }}_{\text{r}}\) as follows,

$$\varrho_r=\varrho_r-\alpha_{r-1}$$	(21)

Where ar-1 is given by (19).

Finally, HF-DIF obtains the indices of the hierarchically selected features from the r-th projected data set Xr by

$${\Omega'}_r={\left({\widehat\Omega}_r\right)}_{\varrho r}$$	(22)

where Ω’r denotes the index list of features that are hierarchically selected from \({X}_{r}\), \({\Omega }_{r}{\prime}\) reveals the feature index relationship between the projected data set Xr and the hierarchically selected data set \(\widetilde{\text{X}}\), \(r=\text{1,2},\dots ,\Gamma\).

Based on (22), it can be inferred that \(\widetilde X\) can be formed not only by \(\widetilde{X}={\left(\widehat{X}\right)}_{\widehat{\Omega }}\) but also by

$$\widetilde x=\left[{\left(x_1\right)}_{{\Omega'}_2,\dots,}{\left(X_\Gamma\right)}_{{\Omega'}_\Gamma}\right]$$	(23)

Furthermore, \({\Omega }{\prime}=\left[{\Omega }_{1}{\prime},{\Omega }_{2}{\prime},\dots , {\Omega }_{\Gamma }{\prime}\right]\) can be sent to the testing stage of HF-DIF which make the projected testing data be directly transformed to the hierarchically selected representation. Therefore, the backtracking of feature indices contributes to establishing a direct mapping from the hierarchical selected data set to the original projected data sets. This is appropriate for both training data set and testing data set. 4.6 Representation-adaptive shrinkage strategy

As illustrated in Section 4.4, shrink coefficients (i.e.,n1,…,nΓ in the first-level feature selection, and n’ in the second-level feature selection) are key parameters in the hierarchical feature selection scheme of HF-DIF for determining the effective dimension of each projected representation member. Instead of relying on fixed or manually tuned shrink coefficients, HF-DIF employs a representation-adaptive shrinkage strategy to determine the optimal values of shrink coefficients. This strategy reuses the integrated feature importance scores that have been obtained by the iFFE module (see (17)) to estimate the values of the shrink coefficients. The determination process for the \(\text{r}\) th projected representation member (subspace) is given as follows:

Step 1 Curve Construction. A mean feature importance curve is constructed in the geometric space \(\left[\left.1/\text{m},1\right)\right.\times \left(\left.\text{0,1}\right]\right.\) based on the sorted integrated feature importance scores \({\text{l}}_{{\uplambda }_{1}\left|\text{r}\right.}>{\text{l}}_{{\uplambda }_{2}\left|\text{r}\right.}>\dots >{\text{l}}_{{\uplambda }_{\text{m}}\left|\text{r}\right.},\text{r}=1,\dots ,\Gamma\) (see Step 1 in Section 4.4). Here, the horizontal axis represents the candidate shrink coefficient \({\upeta }_{\text{r}}\), and the vertical axis represents the normalized average feature importance score \(\text{e}\). The curve is defined by evaluating \(\text{e}\) as a function of \({\upeta }_{\text{r}}\). For any candidate \({\upeta }_{\text{r}}\in \left[\left.1/\text{m},1\right)\right.\),

$$e\left(\eta_r\right)=\left(\left({\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{\left\lfloor\eta_rm\right\rfloor}}{\mathcal l}_{\lambda_k\left|r\right.}\right)/\left\lfloor\eta_rm\right\rfloor-{\mathcal l}_{\lambda_{k\left|r\right.}}\right)/\left({\mathcal l}_{\lambda_1\left|r\right.}\right),\;e\left(\eta_r\right)\in(0,1\rbrack$$	(25)

Step 2 Knee Identification. The Kneedle Algorithm (Irwin and Raghavan 2011) is applied to automatically identify the "elbow point" of the constructed curve. Geometrically, the search aims for the optimal \({\upeta }_{\text{r}}^{*}\) that maximizes the vertical distance from the reference line connecting points \(\left(\text{0,1}\right)\) and \(\left(\text{1,0}\right)\) in the space:

$${\upeta }_{\text{r}}^{*}=\underset{{\upeta }_{\text{r}}\in \left[\left.1/\text{m},1\right)\right.}{\text{argmax}}\left|\text{e}\left({\upeta }_{\text{r}}\right)-\left(1-{\upeta }_{\text{r}}\right)\right|$$	(26)

Step 3 Parameter Assignment. Finally, the number of selected features for the \(\text{r}\) th projected representation member (subspace) is determined by \(\left\lfloor\eta_rm\right\rfloor\).

This strategy ensures that \({\upeta }_{\text{r}}^{*}\) is tailored to the quality of each randomly projected representation member (subspace). For informative subspaces with a slow decay in feature quality, the elbow point appears later in the space, resulting in a larger \({\upeta }_{\text{r}}^{*}\) (as shown in Fig. 9(a)). Conversely, for subspaces dominated by sparse informative features, the curve drops sharply, resulting in a smaller \({\upeta }_{\text{r}}^{*}\) (as shown in Fig. 9(b)).

Fig. 9

The blue curve represents the normalized average feature importance score \(\text{e}\) as a function of the candidate shrink coefficient \({\upeta }_{\text{r}}\). The red dot marks the detected "Elbow Point" which maximizes the distance from the reference line (dashed black). a Dense-informative subspace: an informative subspace exhibiting a slow decay in feature quality, leading to a larger shrink coefficient \({\upeta }_{\text{r}}^{*}\). b Sparse-informative subspace: a subspace dominated by sparse informative features (rapid decay), leading to a smaller shrink coefficient \({\upeta }_{\text{r}}^{*}\)

The same strategy is also applied to the shrink coefficient in the second-level feature selection n’ based on the concatenated selected features. It is worth noting that the Representation-Adaptive Shrinkage strategy imposes negligible computational overhead on the overall HF-DIF framework. The calculation and sorting of integrated feature importance scores involves traversing the isolation trees, which is an intrinsic part of the HF-DIF pipeline regardless of whether a fixed or adaptive \({\upeta }_{\text{r}}\) is used. Thus, no additional cost is incurred here. The adaptive determination of \({\upeta }_{\text{r}}\) requires computing the mean importance curve (\(\text{O}\left(\text{m}\right)\)) and finding the elbow point (\(\text{O}\left(\text{m}\right)\) (Irwin and Raghavan 2011)). Since the number of projected features \(\text{m}\) is generally significantly smaller than the sample size, i.e., \(\text{m}\ll \text{N}\), the total cost of this adaptive procedure is linear with respect to the projected feature dimension \(\text{m}\).

4.7 Algorithm of HF-DIF

This section summarizes the execution procedures of the proposed Hierarchical Feature-selected Deep Isolation Forest (HF-DIF) using pseudo-code algorithms. Algorithm 1 formalizes the overall training process, incorporating deep random projection, representation-wise iForest construction, global concatenation, and the index backtracking mechanism that maps the globally selected features back to their original projection subspaces. Algorithm 2 serves as the core evaluation module in the training process of HF-DIF. It first executes the iForest-based Feature Evaluator (iFFE) to compute integrated feature importance scores, and subsequently employs the representation-adaptive shrinkage strategy to dynamically determine the optimal truncation threshold for feature retention. Algorithm 3 details the representation-adaptive shrinkage strategy. It automatically identifies the mathematical "elbow point" on the feature importance curve to dynamically determine the optimal feature selection ratio, effectively eliminating the need for manual parameter tuning. Algorithm 4 outlines the precise index backtracking procedure. It maps the globally selected feature indices from the concatenated space back to their original projected subspaces, establishing the direct index-based lookup mechanism required for the highly efficient testing phase. Algorithm 5 specifies the testing procedure of HF-DIF. It utilizes the fixed random networks and backtracked feature-index lists to perform feature selection via an efficient look-up table operation, thereby enabling highly efficient anomaly scoring via the final detection iForest, which is built upon the globally selected anomaly-discriminant features.

Such distributional characteristics indicate that, after deep random projection and hierarchical iFFE selection, the resulting features exhibit enhanced anomaly-normal separability, which is consistent with the theoretical analysis presented in Section 5.4.

The hierarchical feature-selection mechanism in HF-DIF relies on two complementary components:

Deep random projections generate a rich variety of projected feature spaces, thereby substantially enhancing representation diversity and increasing the chance that some projections expose imbalance patterns between normal samples and anomalies.

The iForest-based Feature Evaluator (iFFE) uses the Imbalance Branch Coefficient as the basis for feature selection; in other words, features that do not exhibit branch-imbalance characteristics are not favored and are unlikely to be selected by HF-DIF.

These two components are used jointly in HF-DIF. Deep random projections diversify the representations, while the imbalance-sensitive feature evaluator assigns higher scores to features that exhibit branch imbalance between normal samples and anomalies. As a result, the features ultimately selected by HF-DIF predominantly show skewed, imbalance-driven distributions that are conducive to early isolation of anomalies. For generic, smooth feature distributions that do not induce noticeable imbalance between normal samples and anomalies, their contribution to isolation-based anomaly detection is limited and their potential to be exploited by isolation-based detectors is low. In such cases, HF-DIF is designed to down-weight or ignore these features.

In addition, although the proposed HF-DIF is inspired by the Depth-based Isolation Forest Feature Importance (DIFFI) (Carletti et al. 2023), it is not a direct application or extension of DIFFI. The key differences between HF-DIF and DIFFI are summarized as follows.

DIFFI evaluates feature importance directly on raw features and is primarily designed to improve the interpretability of Isolation Forest (iForest) models, while the proposed IFFE module in HF-DIF operates on deep random projected features produced by randomly initialized neural mappings and therefore does not preserve explicit physical semantics of the original variables. Consequently, interpretability is not the primary objective of IFFE; instead, it focuses on anomaly-detection-oriented feature evaluation and selection, aiming to identify projected features that contribute most effectively to anomaly–normal separability.

DIFFI quantifies feature importance mainly for interpreting iForest behavior on a given feature space, whereas HF-DIF further integrates feature-importance evaluation with a representation-adaptive shrinkage strategy for projected subspaces. Consequently, DIFFI neither determines the retained feature dimension of each projected subspace adaptively nor provides an explicit criterion for feature selection or filtering based on each feature’s contribution to anomaly–normal separability, while HF-DIF reuses feature-importance estimates to adaptively determine retained dimensions and to preserve features with greater contributions to anomaly–normal separability while filtering out low-contribution ones.

DIFFI does not consider the heterogeneous effects of features from different projected subspaces on anomaly detection, nor does it provide a scheme for globally refining locally selected candidates into a selectively consolidated set of anomaly-discriminative features. By contrast, the hierarchical feature-selection scheme in HF-DIF explicitly addresses cross-subspace heterogeneity through a two-level design, in which features are first selected within each projected subspace (representation member) and then further refined from a global perspective. This enables HF-DIF to amplify the influence of subspaces that are more beneficial to anomaly–normal separability while attenuating the impact of less suitable ones, leading to a globally optimized feature subset for unsupervised anomaly detection.

5.2 Computational complexity analysis

Suppose the randomly initialized neural networks used in HF-DIF for data projection have \(\text{d}\) input nodes, \(\text{L}\) hidden layers and \(\text{m}\) output nodes, where the \(\text{l}\)-th hidden layer contains \({\text{n}}_{\text{l}}\) nodes (\(1\le \text{l}\le \text{L}\)). The computational complexity are analyzed as follows.

The computational complexity of deep projection

Similar with DIF (Xu et al. 2023a), the computational complexity of the deep projection via the randomly initialized neural networks is \(O\left(\Gamma \times N\times \left(d{n}_{1}+{n}_{L}m+\sum_{l=1}^{L-1}{n}_{l}{n}_{l+1}\right)\right)\) where \(\text{N}\) is the number of training data, \(\Gamma\) is the number of random representation members (subspaces). If there is just one hidden layer (i.e., \(\text{L}=1\),) in the neural networks, the computational complexity of projection becomes \(O\left(\Gamma \times N\times \left(d{n}_{1}+{n}_{1}m\right)\right)\). If there is no hidden layer in the neural networks (i.e., \(\text{L}=0\), just input layer nodes, output layer nodes, and fully connected weights with random initialization), as represented by (1), the computational complexity of projection becomes \(\text{O}\left(\Gamma \times \text{N}\times \text{md}\right)\)

The computational complexity of iForest building

In the training stage, HF-DIF builds \(\Gamma\) iForests with \(\Gamma\) subsets sampled from randomly projected representations (as described in Section 4.2), respectively. According to the analysis of iForest (Liu et al. 2012), the computational complexity of building an iForest is \(\text{O}\left(\text{K}\times\uppsi {\text{log}}_{2}\uppsi \right)\) where \(\text{K}\) is the number of iTrees in each iForest, \(\uppsi\) is the subsampling size. Therefore, HF-DIF takes \(O\left(\Gamma \times K\times \psi {log}_{2}\psi \right)\) computational complexity in the building process of \(\Gamma\) iForests in HF-DIF.

The computational complexity of the IFFE module

The IFFE module of HF-DIF implements the iForest-based feature evaluation using four steps (Section 4.3). First, each built iForest evaluates its corresponding projected training data set and partitions it into the anomaly data subset and the data subset. According to the analysis of iForest (Liu et al. 2012), the computational complexity of the first step is \(O\left(\Gamma \times K{\times Nlog}_{2}\psi \right)\) where \(\text{N}\) is the size of training dataset. Second, based on the intermediate results of built iForest, the computational complexity of imbalance degrees is \(O\left(\Gamma \times N\times K{log}_{2}\psi \right)\). Third, the computational complexity of average visiting count and terminate depth is similar with that of imbalance degrees, i.e., \(O\left(\Gamma \times N\times K{log}_{2}\psi \right)\). Fourth, the computational complexity of feature importance scores \(O\left(\Gamma \times m\times K\right)\). Then, the overall computational complexity of the \(\Gamma\) IFFE modules is \(O\left(\Gamma \times N\times K{log}_{2}\psi +\Gamma \times m\times K\right)=O\left(\Gamma \times K\times \left(N{log}_{2}\psi +m\right)\right)\).

The computational complexity of hierarchical feature selection

The hierarchical feature selection involves four steps (Section 4.4). First, the first-level feature selection for each projected representation dataset is achieved by the sorting of \(\text{m}\) features, so the computational complexity is \(O\left(\Gamma\times m\log_2m\right)\). Second, the computational complexity of feature concatenation can be neglected. Third, the second-level feature selection is implemented by one IFFE module for \(\text{N}\) data with \(\left\lfloor\eta m\right\rfloor\Gamma\) features, so the computational complexity is \(O\left(K\times\left(N\log_2\Psi+\left\lfloor\eta m\right\rfloor\Gamma\right)+\left\lfloor\eta m\right\rfloor\Gamma\log_2\left\lfloor nm\right\rfloor\Gamma\right)\). Fourth, the computational complexity of the backtracking operation can also be neglected compared with the first and the third step. Usually, Therefore, the overall computational complexity of the hierarchical feature selection is \(O\left(K\times\left(N\log_2\Psi+\left\lfloor\eta m\right\rfloor\Gamma\right)+\left\lfloor\eta m\right\rfloor\Gamma\log_2\left\lfloor nm\right\rfloor\Gamma\right)\).

In summary, the computational complexity during the training stage of HF-DIF is approximately O(N×d×Γ×(m+K)). It has linear complexity w.r.t. data size, dimensionality (input dimensionality or projected dimensionality), and ensemble size, which inherits the desired scalability from DIF.

During the testing stage, HF-DIF takes O(Γ×N’×md) computational complexity in the data projection where N’ is the number of testing data. Depending upon the index set derived by the backtracking operation, HF-DIF can directly determine the final selected \(\left\lfloor\eta m\right\rfloor\Gamma\) features and obtain the anomaly scores of testing data. Based on the analysis of Liu et al. (2012), the evaluation complexity of iForest is \(O\left({N}{\prime}K{log}_{2}\psi \right)\). Hence, the computational complexity of during the testing stage of HF-DIF is \(O\left(N'\left(\Gamma\times md+K\log_2\psi\right)\right)\).

The power of HF-DIF depends on (i) the inheritance of the advantages of DIF (i.e., the strong and diverse representation ability without optimization), and (ii) the introduction of the hierarchical feature selection scheme based on iForest.

Neural networks have strong representation power, even for randomly initialized networks (Xu et al. 2023a). DIF utilizes the casually initialized neural networks to generate randomly projected feature representations, which improves anomaly detection performance without adding too much additional computational complexity. The randomness is able to enhance the diversity of feature representations, yet it could also weaken the stability of the anomaly detector. This is because the diverse randomly projected features are not definitely consistent with the objective of anomaly detection, and it is difficult to avoid generating features that confuse the specificity of anomalous points. DIF has to maintain a large-scale representation ensemble and iTree ensemble, which aims to reduce the decision variance of DIF caused by the feature randomness. A straightforward and effect idea is to select the feature candidates, which are conducive to distinguishing anomalous points, from the large scale randomly projected features. Moreover, the feature selection method should ideally leverage the advantages of iForest.

The proposed HF-DIF not only inherits the random representation ensemble, but also employs the hierarchical feature selection scheme based on iForest. The hierarchical feature selection scheme takes into account the randomly projected features from both intra-representation member and inter-representation perspectives, and it leverages the advantages of isolation-based models (e.g., iForest) to identify suitable features that reveal the distinctiveness of anomalies.

5.3 Theoretical analysis

It is a straightforward principle that, for anomaly detection models, better detection performance can be achieved when there is a greater separation of anomaly scores between anomalies and normal objects, as this facilitates more accurate identification of anomalies within large datasets (see Definition 1 and Lemma 1).

Definition 1 (Anomaly Score Separation)

Given a dataset X consisting of anomaly objects and normal objects, an anomaly detection model that assigns an anomaly score s(x) to each object x ∈ X, let \(\text{A}\) denote the set of anomaly objects in X, and N denote the set of normal objects in X, and they follow X=A∪N, and A∩N=∅. The anomaly score separation is defined as the difference between the expected anomaly scores for anomaly objects and normal objects, i.e.,

$$\text{S}=\text{E}\left[\text{s}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\right)\right]-\text{E}\left[\text{s}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\right]$$	(27)

where \({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\) denotes an anomaly object in \(\text{A}\), and \({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\) denotes a normal object in \(\text{N}\), \(\text{E}\left[\text{s}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\right)\right]\) and \(\text{E}\left[\text{s}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\right]\) denote the expected anomaly scores for anomaly objects \({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\in \text{A}\) and normal objects \({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\in \text{N}\), respectively.

Lemma 1 (Detectability Monotonically Increases with Anomaly Score Separation)

Suppose the anomaly score distributions for normal and anomaly objects are continuous and have finite variance. Then, as the anomaly score separation \(\text{S}\) (as defined in Definition 1) increases, the probability \(\text{P}\left(\text{s}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\right)>\text{s}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\right)\) that the anomaly detection model correctly distinguishes an anomaly object \({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\) from a normal object \({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\) increases monotonically.

Proof

Let \({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\) denote an anomaly object randomly sampled from \(\text{A}\), and \({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\) denote a normal object randomly sampled from \(\text{N}\). Following Definition 1, the anomaly score separation is defined by \(\text{S}=\text{E}\left[\text{s}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\right)\right]-\text{E}\left[\text{s}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\right]\).

Define the random variable \(\text{V}=\text{s}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\right)-\text{s}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\), which has expectation \(\text{E}\left[\text{V}\right]=\text{S}\) and finite variance \(Var\left(\text{V}\right)={\upsigma }^{2}\), by assumption. Then, the probability that the anomaly detection model assigns a higher score to an anomaly object than to a normal object is given by

$$\text{P}\left(\text{s}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\right)>\text{s}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\right)=\text{P}\left(\text{V}>0\right)$$

By Chebyshev’s inequality, \(\forall\upepsilon >0\),

$$\text{P}\left(\left|\text{V}-\text{E}\left[\text{V}\right]\right|\ge\upepsilon \right)=\text{P}\left(\left|\text{V}-\text{S}\right|\ge\upepsilon \right)\le {\upsigma }^{2}/{\upepsilon }^{2}$$	(28)

Take \(\upepsilon =\text{S}\), then obtain

$$\text{P}\left(\left|\text{V}-\text{S}\right|\ge \text{S}\right)\le {\upsigma }^{2}/{\text{S}}^{2}$$	(29)

Note that \(\left|\text{V}-\text{S}\right|\ge \text{S}\) if and only if \(\text{V}\le 0\) or \(\text{V}\ge 2\text{S}\), so

$$\text{P}\left(\left|\text{V}-\text{S}\right|\ge \text{S}\right)=\text{P}\left(\text{V}\le 0\right)+\text{P}\left(\text{V}\ge 2\text{S}\right)$$	(30)

Thus,

$$\text{P}\left(\text{V}\le 0\right)\le \text{P}\left(\left|\text{V}-\text{S}\right|\ge \text{S}\right)\le {\upsigma }^{2}/{\text{S}}^{2}$$	(31)

Since \(\text{P}\left(\text{V}>0\right)=1-\text{ P}\left(\text{V}\le 0\right)\), From (29), it follows that

$$\text{P}\left(\text{V}>0\right)\ge 1-{\upsigma }^{2}/{\text{S}}^{2}$$	(32)

Recall that \(\text{V}=\text{s}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\right)-\text{s}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\). Thus,

$$\text{P}\left(\text{s}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\right)>\text{s}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\right)\ge 1-{\upsigma }^{2}/{\text{S}}^{2}$$	(33)

As \(\text{S}\) increases, the lower bound of \(\text{P}\left(\text{s}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\right)>\text{s}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\right)\), i.e., \(1-\frac{{\upsigma }^{2}}{{\text{S}}^{2}}\), increases monotonically. Therefore, the probability that the anomaly detection model correctly distinguishes an anomaly object from a normal object increases monotonically with the anomaly score separation.

Based on Anomaly Score Separation (Definition 1 and Lemma 1), the anomaly detection advantage of HF-DIF over DIF is analyzed by investigating how their distinct model mechanisms affect the anomaly score function.

Definition 2 (Path-Length-Based Factor and Deviation-Based Factor of anomaly score function)

Without loss of generality, for a given iTree, let Z(x) denote the set of nodes traversed by x from the root node to the termination node of iTree, g(x) denote the averaged deviation of x in iTree, defined by

$$g\left(x\right)=\frac{1}{\left|Z\left(x\right)\right|}\sum_{z\in Z\left(x\right)}\left|{x}^{\left({j}_{z}\right)}-{v}_{z}\right|$$	(34)

where \({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\) denotes the feature index corresponding to the z-th node of iTree, \({v}_{z}\sim Uniform\left[min\left({x}^{\left({j}_{z}\right)}\right),max\left({x}^{\left({j}_{z}\right)}\right)\right]\) denotes the splitting threshold for the z-th node of iTree, where \({\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}\) is the \({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\)-th feature value of \(\text{x}\).

Define the anomaly score function as

$$s\left(x\right)={2}^{-E\left(\left|Z\left(x\right)\right|\right)/c\left(n\right)}\times E\left[g\left(x\right)\right]$$	(35)

where \(\text{c}\left(\text{n}\right)\) denotes the normalizing factor.

Based on (33), define the Path-Length-Based Factor a(x) and the Deviation-Based Factor β(x) as follows,

$$\alpha \left(x\right)={2}^{-E\left(\left|Z\left(x\right)\right|\right)/c\left(n\right)} and \beta \left(x\right)=E\left[g\left(x\right)\right]$$	(36)

Then, the anomaly score function s(x) in (33) can be rewritten as s(x)=a(x)β(x). Based on Definition 1, The anomaly score separation can be rewritten as

$$S= \mathbb{E}\left[s\left(x_\alpha\right)\right]- \mathbb{E}\left[s\left(x_n\right)\right]= \mathbb{E}\left[\alpha\left(x_\alpha\right)\right] \mathbb{E}\left[\beta\left(x_\alpha\right)\right]- \mathbb{E}\left[\beta\left(x_n\right)\right]$$	(37)
Lemma 2 (The Differential Effects of HF-DIF and DIF on the Path-Length-Based Factor)

Let \({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\in \text{A}\) and xn∈N denote anomaly and normal objects in anomaly set \(\text{A}\) and normal set \(\text{N}\), respectively. Suppose the feature dimension of object is \(\text{d}\). As defined in Definition 2, a(xa) and a(xn) denote the Path-Length-Based Factors on xa and xn, respectively. In comparison to DIF, HF-DIF enhances the expected value E[a(xa)] for anomaly object xa, and simultaneously suppresses \(\mathbb{E}\left[\beta\left(x_n\right)\right]\) for normal samples xn.

Proof

The proof includes two parts corresponding to \({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\in \text{A}\) and \({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\in \text{N}\), respectively.

(a)

Anomaly Object Scenario

For anomaly objects \({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\in \text{A}\), HF-DIF shortens the expected path length by leveraging feature selection. In HF-DIF, feature selection is performed based on the iForest-based feature evaluator (iFFE), which scores each feature by:

The frequency with which it is used to split anomaly objects;

The early termination depth of anomaly splits in that feature;

The imbalance between anomaly and normal counts induced by its partitions.

As a result, HF-DIF selects a feature index subset \(\Omega \subset \left\{\text{1,2},\dots ,\text{d}\right\}\) in which anomalies are:

More easily isolated;

More frequently encountered near the top of the iTree;

More directionally separated from normal objects.

Thus, in HF-DIF, anomaly objects are often split off early using the selected strong anomaly-sensitive features. In contrast, DIF selects splitting features uniformly at random from \(\left\{\text{1,2},\dots ,\text{d}\right\}\), without regard to anomaly separability. Therefore, it holds:

$$\begin{array}{c} \mathbb{E}\left[\left|Z^{HF-DIF}\left(x_\alpha\right)\right|\right]\leq \mathbb{E}\left[\left|Z^{DIF}\left(x_\alpha\right)\right|\right]\Rightarrow2^{- \mathbb{E}\left[Z^{HF-DIF\left(x_\alpha\right)}\right]^{/c\left(n\right)}\geq2^{- \mathbb{E}}\left[\left|Z^{DIF}\left(x_\alpha\right)\right|\right]/c\left(n\right)}\\\alpha^{HF-DIF}\left(x_\alpha\right)\geq\alpha^{DIF}\left(x_\alpha\right)\\ \mathbb{E}\left[\alpha^{HF-DIF}\left(x_\alpha\right)\right]\geq \mathbb{E}\left[\alpha^{DIF}\left(x_\alpha\right)\right]\end{array}$$	(38)

where \({\text{Z}}^{HF-DIF}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\right)\) and \({\text{Z}}^{DIF}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\right)\) are the traverse node sets for \({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\in \text{A}\) achieved by HF-DIF and DIF, respectively. \(\text{E}\left[\left|{\text{Z}}^{HF-DIF}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\right)\right|\right]\) and \(\text{E}\left[\left|{\text{Z}}^{DIF}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\right)\right|\right]\) are the expected values of \({\text{Z}}^{HF-DIF}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\right)\) and \({\text{Z}}^{DIF}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\right)\), respectively.

(b)

Normal Object Scenario

HF-DIF partitions objects into distinct branches of the isolation tree (iTree) along features selected by iFFE. Each selected feature typically exhibits a highly skewed distribution: anomalous objects tend to appear in the tail region, distant from the central mass of the distribution, whereas normal objects concentrate around the core region. Such skewed distributions of selected features facilitate the early isolation of anomaly objects \({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\) at shallow iTree depths in HF-DIF. However, isolating a normal sample from other normal objects generally requires more splitting operations, resulting in traversal through additional iTree nodes and, consequently, longer path lengths.

Unlike HF-DIF, DIF selects features randomly across the entire feature space. As a result, DIF inevitably performs splits operations along the features with relatively flat distributions, i.e., those lacking strong skewness. Under such conditions, anomaly objects are less likely to be early isolated. Meanwhile, normal objects require fewer splits (small path lengths) than in scenarios where feature values exhibit high concentration. Therefore, the formula holds:

$$\begin{array}{c}E\left[\left|Z^{HF-DIF}\left(x_n\right)\right|\right]\geq E\left[\left|Z^{DIF}\left(x_n\right)\right|\right]\Rightarrow2^{-E\left[\left|Z^{HF-DIF}\left(x_n\right)\right|\right]/c\left(n\right)}\leq2^{-E\left[\left|Z^{DIF}\left(x_n\right)\right|\right]/c\left(n\right)}\\\Rightarrow\;\alpha^{HF-DIF}\left(x_n\right)\leq\alpha^{DIF}\left(x_n\right)\Rightarrow E\left[\alpha^{HF-DIF}\left(x_n\right)\right]\leq E\left[\alpha^{DIF}\left(x_n\right)\right]\end{array}$$	(39)

where \({\text{Z}}^{HF-DIF}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\) and \({\text{Z}}^{DIF}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\) denote the traverse node sets for \({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\in \text{N}\) achieved by HF-DIF and DIF, respectively. \(\text{E}\left[\left|{\text{Z}}^{HF-DIF}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\right|\right]\) and \(\text{E}\left[\left|{\text{Z}}^{DIF}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\right|\right]\) denote the expected values of \({\text{Z}}^{HF-DIF}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\) and \({\text{Z}}^{DIF}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\), respectively.

Lemma 3 (The Differential Effects of HF-DIF and DIF on the Deviation-Based Factor)

Let \({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\in \text{A}\) and \({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\in \text{N}\) denote anomaly and normal objects in anomaly set \(\text{A}\) and normal set \(\text{N}\), respectively. As defined in Definition 2, \(\upbeta \left({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\right)\) and \(\upbeta \left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\) denote the Deviation-Based Factors on \({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\) and \({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\), respectively. In comparison to DIF, HF-DIF enhances the expected value \(\text{E}\left[\upbeta \left({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\right)\right]\) for normal object \({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\), and simultaneously suppresses \(\text{E}\left[\upbeta \left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\right]\) for normal samples \({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\).

Proof

Based on Definition 2, let \(\text{Z}\left(\text{x}\right)\) denote the set of nodes traversed by \(\text{x}\) from the root node to the termination node of iTree, \({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\) denote the feature index corresponding to the z-th node of iTree, \(\text{z}\in \text{Z}\left(\text{x}\right)\), \({\text{v}}_{\text{z}}\sim Uniform\left[min\left({\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}\right),max\left({\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}\right)\right]\) denotes the splitting threshold for the z-th node of iTree, where \({\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}\) is the \({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\)-th feature value of \(\text{x}\). Then, the expected deviation between \({\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}\) and \({\text{v}}_{\text{z}}\) is given by

$${\updelta }_{\text{z}}\left(\text{x}\right)=\text{E}\left[\left|{\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}-{\text{v}}_{\text{z}}\right|\right]$$	(40)
(a)

Normal Object Scenario

As discussed in Lemma 2, each selected feature in HF-DIF exhibits a highly skewed distribution, where normal objects are concentrated around the central mass of the distribution. In contrast, DIF selects features randomly across the entire feature space, inevitably including features with relatively flat distributions, where normal objects are more evenly dispersed. Consequently, HF-DIF generally requires more splits to separate a normal object from other normal ones. It follows that, in HF-DIF, the splitting thresholds intersect dense regions of normal objects more frequently than in DIF. Therefore, the expected deviation between a sampled object's feature value and the splitting threshold is typically less than or equal to that in DIF:

$$\begin{array}{lc}\forall x_n\in N,\;z\in Z^{HF-DIF}\left(x_n\right),\;z'\in Z^{DIF}\left(x_n\right),\\ \mathbb {E}\left[\delta_z\left(x_n\right)\right]\leq\;\mathbb {E}\left[\delta_{z'}\left(x_n\right)\right]\Rightarrow\;\mathbb {E}\left[\frac1{\left|Z^{HF-DIF}\left(x_n\right)\right|}\sum_{z\in Z^{HF-DIF}\left(x_n\right)}\;\mathbb {E}\left[\delta_z\left(x_n\right)\right]\right]\\\Rightarrow\;\mathbb {E}\left[g^{HF-DIF}\left(x_n\right)\right]\leq\;\mathbb {E}\left[g^{DIF}\left(x_n\right)\right]\Rightarrow\mathbb {E}\left[\beta^{HF-DIF}\left(x_n\right)\right]\leq\;\mathbb {E}\left[\beta^{DIF}\left(x_n\right)\right]\end{array}\leq\mathbb {E}\left[\frac1{\left|Z^{DIF}\left(x_n\right)\right|}\sum_{z'\in Z^{DIF}\left(x_n\right)}\;\mathbb {E}\left[\delta_{z'}\left(x_n\right)\right]\right]$$

where \({\text{Z}}^{HF-DIF}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\) and \({\text{Z}}^{DIF}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\) are defined in Lemma 2, \({\text{g}}^{HF-DIF}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\) and \({\text{g}}^{DIF}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\) denote the averaged deviations of \({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\) in iTrees of HF-DIF and DIF, respectively. \({\upbeta }^{HF-DIF}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\) and \({\upbeta }^{DIF}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\right)\) denote Deviation-Based Factors for \({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\) achieved by HF-DIF and DIF, respectively.

(b)

Anomaly Object Scenario

Since \({\text{v}}_{\text{z}}\sim Uniform\left[min\left({\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}\right),max\left({\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}\right)\right]\) (given in (38)), the density function of \({\text{v}}_{\text{z}}\) can be denoted by \(1/\left(max\left({\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}\right)-min\left({\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}\right)\right)\). Then, the expected deviation \({\updelta }_{\text{z}}\left(\text{x}\right)\) between \(\text{x}\) and \({\text{v}}_{\text{z}}\) (defined in (38)) can be further derived as follows:

$$\begin{array}{c}{\updelta }_{\text{z}}\left(\text{x}\right)=\text{E}\left[\left|{\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}-{\text{v}}_{\text{z}}\right|\right]={\int }_{{\text{a}}_{\text{z}}}^{{\text{b}}_{\text{z}}}\left|{\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}-{\text{v}}_{\text{z}}\right|\cdot \frac{1}{{\text{b}}_{\text{z}}-{\text{a}}_{\text{z}}}{\text{dv}}_{\text{z}}=\frac{1}{{\text{b}}_{\text{z}}-{\text{a}}_{\text{z}}}{\int }_{{\text{a}}_{\text{z}}}^{{\text{b}}_{\text{z}}}\left|{\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}-{\text{v}}_{\text{z}}\right|{\text{dv}}_{\text{z}}\\ =\frac{1}{{\text{b}}_{\text{z}}-{\text{a}}_{\text{z}}}\left({\int }_{{\text{a}}_{\text{z}}}^{{\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}}\left({\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}-{\text{v}}_{\text{z}}\right){\text{dv}}_{\text{z}}+{\int }_{{\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}}^{{\text{b}}_{\text{z}}}\left({\text{v}}_{\text{z}}{-\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}\right){\text{dv}}_{\text{z}}\right)\\ =\left({\left({\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}\right)}^{2}-{\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}\left({\text{a}}_{\text{z}}+{\text{b}}_{\text{z}}\right)+\frac{{\text{a}}_{\text{z}}^{2}+{\text{b}}_{\text{z}}^{2}}{2}\right)/\left({\text{b}}_{\text{z}}-{\text{a}}_{\text{z}}\right)\end{array}$$	(41)

where \(\text{z}\in \text{Z}\left(\text{x}\right)\), \({\text{b}}_{\text{z}}=max\left({\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}\right)\), and \({\text{a}}_{\text{z}}=min\left({\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}\right)\).

Set \(\text{m}=\left({\text{a}}_{\text{z}}+{\text{b}}_{\text{z}}\right)/2\), rewrite (39) as follows

$${\updelta }_{\text{z}}\left(\text{x}\right)=\text{E}\left[\left|{\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}-{\text{v}}_{\text{z}}\right|\right]=\frac{1}{{\text{b}}_{\text{z}}-{\text{a}}_{\text{z}}}{\left({\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}-\text{m}\right)}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left({\text{b}}_{\text{z}}-{\text{a}}_{\text{z}}\right)$$	(42)

From (40), it can be inferred that the greater the deviation between \({\text{x}}^{\left({\text{j}}_{\text{z}}\right)}\) and \(\text{m}\), the larger the expected deviation \({\updelta }_{\text{z}}\left(\text{x}\right)\). As discussed in Lemma 2, HF-DIF performs feature selection based on iFFE. Each selected feature in HF-DIF typically exhibits a highly skewed distribution where anomaly objects tend to lie in the tail region, distant from the central mass of the distribution. Nevertheless, DIF selects features uniformly at random from the entire feature space, which inevitably includes features with relatively flat distributions. In such cases, anomalies are not significantly concentrated in tail regions, and their distributions are not substantially separated from the central mass of normal data.

Therefore, under HF-DIF, the feature value of anomaly object is statistically farther from the midpoint than under DIF. Namely, for anomaly object \({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\in \text{A}\), the expected deviation between feature value and threshold value of HF-DIF is larger than or equal to that of DIF:

$$\begin{array}{c}\forall {x}_{a}\in A,z\in {Z}^{HF-DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right),{z}{\prime}\in {Z}^{DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\\ E\left[{\delta }_{z}^{HF-DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right]\ge E\left[{\delta }_{{z}{\prime}}^{DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right]\\ \begin{array}{c}\Rightarrow E\left[\frac{1}{\left|{Z}^{HF-DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right|}\sum_{z\in {Z}^{HF-DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)}E\left[{\delta }_{z}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right]\right]\ge E\left[\frac{1}{\left|{Z}^{DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right|}\sum_{{z}{\prime}\in {Z}^{DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)}E\left[{\delta }_{{z}{\prime}}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right]\right]\\ \Rightarrow E\left[{g}^{HF-DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right]\ge E\left[{g}^{DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right]\Rightarrow E\left[{\beta }^{HF-DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right]\ge E\left[{\beta }^{DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right]\end{array}\end{array}$$

Based on Lemma 1 to Lemma 3, Theorem 1 is proposed to prove that HF-DIF outperforms DIF in anomaly detection capability.

Theorem 1 (Anomaly Detection Advantage of HF-DIF over DIF)

Let \({\text{S}}^{HF-DIF}\) and \({\text{S}}^{DIF}\) denote the anomaly score separations achieved by HF-DIF and DIF, respectively. It follows from Lemma 1 that if \({\text{S}}^{HF-DIF}\ge {\text{S}}^{DIF}\), then HF-DIF possesses superior capability for detecting anomaly objects compared to DIF.

Proof

Based on Definition 2, \({\text{S}}^{HF-DIF}\) and \({\text{S}}^{DIF}\) can be defined by

$${S}^{HF-DIF}=E\left[{\alpha }^{HF-DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right]E\left[{\beta }^{HF-DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right]-E\left[{\alpha }^{HF-DIF}\left({x}_{n}\right)\right]E\left[{\beta }^{HF-DIF}\left({x}_{n}\right)\right]$$
$${S}^{DIF}=E\left[{\alpha }^{DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right]E\left[{\beta }^{DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right]-E\left[{\alpha }^{DIF}\left({x}_{n}\right)\right]E\left[{\beta }^{DIF}\left({x}_{n}\right)\right]$$

where aHF-DIF(xa),a HF-DIF(xn), βHF-DIF(xa), and βHF-DIF(xn) denote the Path-Length-Based Factors and the Deviation-Based Factors achieved by HF-DIF for \({\text{x}}_{\text{a}}\) and \({\text{x}}_{\text{n}}\), respectively. aDIF(xa), aDIF(xn), βDIF(xa) and βDIF(xn) and denote the Path-Length-Based Factors and the Deviation-Based Factors achieved by DIF for xa and xn, respectively.

By Lemma 2, it holds that E[aHF-DIF(xa)]≥E[aDIF(xa)] and E[aHF-DIF(xn)] ≤ E[aDIF(xn)]. According to Lemma 3, E[βHF-DIF(xa)]≥E[βDIF(xa)]and E[βHF-DIF(xn)]≤E[βDIF(xn)]. Then, the following formulas are further derived:

$$E\left[{\alpha }^{HF-DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right]E\left[{\beta }^{HF-DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right]\ge E\left[{\alpha }^{DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right]E\left[{\beta }^{DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right]$$	(43)
$$E\left[{\alpha }^{HF-DIF}\left({x}_{n}\right)\right]E\left[{\beta }^{HF-DIF}\left({x}_{n}\right)\right]\le E\left[{\alpha }^{DIF}\left({x}_{n}\right)\right]E\left[{\beta }^{DIF}\left({x}_{n}\right)\right]$$	(44)

Combining (41) and (42) yields the following result:

$$E\left[{\alpha }^{HF-DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right]E\left[{\beta }^{HF-DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right]-E\left[{\alpha }^{HF-DIF}\left({x}_{n}\right)\right]E\left[{\beta }^{HF-DIF}\left({x}_{n}\right)\right]\ge E\left[{\alpha }^{DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right]E\left[{\beta }^{DIF}\left({x}_{a}\right)\right]-E\left[{\alpha }^{DIF}\left({x}_{n}\right)\right]E\left[{\beta }^{DIF}\left({x}_{n}\right)\right]$$

Then,

$${\text{S}}^{HF-DIF}\ge {\text{S}}^{DIF}$$	(45)

By Lemma 1,

$${\text{S}}^{HF-DIF}\ge {\text{S}}^{DIF}$$

implies that HF-DIF possesses superior capability for detecting anomaly objects compared to DIF.

5.4 Limitation and potential direction

A limitation of the current theoretical analysis is that it relies on the assumption that the features selected by HF-DIF consistently exhibit skewed, imbalance-exposing distributions that facilitate early isolation of anomalies. This assumption is unlikely to hold uniformly across all datasets or all possible feature subsets. In practice, the hierarchical feature-selection mechanism of HF-DIF is explicitly biased toward such features, whenever they arise in the projected feature pool. Consequently, HF-DIF is expected to achieve greater improvements on datasets whose projected features exhibit informative, imbalance-exposing structures. On datasets where such structures are weak or difficult to obtain, the performance improvement brought by HF-DIF is relatively small.

At present, the theoretical analysis and algorithmic design of HF-DIF are developed under the canonical iForest assumption that split thresholds are selected uniformly at random within the range of the chosen feature. This choice keeps the computational complexity low and aligns HF-DIF with standard isolation-forest implementations, but it does not exploit the detailed structure of the empirical distributions of the selected features. A promising direction for future research is therefore to develop optimal, or at least more data-adaptive, split-selection mechanisms that leverage the imbalance-exposing distributions uncovered by the hierarchical feature-selection scheme in HF-DIF. Such mechanisms could bias split thresholds toward regions where anomalies are more likely to be isolated early or where normal data are sparse, while still preserving the scalability and simplicity of isolation-based ensembles. Extending the current theoretical framework to cover these more sophisticated split-selection strategies, and systematically studying the trade-off between computational cost, robustness, and detection accuracy, constitutes an important avenue for future work.

Evaluation metrics

The choice of performance metrics is crucial for obtaining meaningful and comparable assessments of model performance across datasets, targets, and tasks. In regression and forecasting scenarios—particularly time-series prediction and commodity/resource price forecasting—normalized error metrics such as relative root mean square error (RMSE) are widely recommended because they mitigate the influence of differing target scales and units, thereby enabling fair cross-dataset and cross-model comparisons (Xu and Zhang 2023b, 2023c; Jin and Xu 2025f). In contrast, unsupervised anomaly detection does not aim to predict a continuous target value, and thus lacks a natural notion of residual error (prediction minus ground truth) on which RMSE or normalized error metrics rely. Instead, each unsupervised anomaly detection model outputs a real-valued anomaly score for every sample, and evaluation focuses on whether these scores rank anomalous samples ahead of normal ones, without reference to a fixed prediction scale or a true continuous target.

The anomaly-detection literature predominantly adopts ranking-based metrics—specifically, the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC-ROC), the Area Under the Precision–Recall Curve (AUC-PR), and Precision@K—which directly measure models' ability to separate anomalous samples from normal samples (Sørbø and Ruocco 2024; Ma et al. 2023). Ground-truth labels are used solely at evaluation time to compute these metrics from the anomaly-score ranking, while model training itself remains entirely unsupervised.

AUC-ROC evaluates how well a model ranks anomalous samples above normal ones across all possible score thresholds. It is computed by integrating the ROC curve formed from the true-positive and false-positive rates as the threshold varies. Because it is independent of any fixed operating point and is insensitive to changes in class imbalance, AUC-ROC provides a global assessment of separability and is widely regarded as a reliable indicator of a model’s overall ranking quality. Improvements in AUC-ROC therefore signify that anomalies consistently receive higher scores than normal instances under most threshold settings, reflecting stronger global discriminative ability. Higher AUC-ROC implies more reliable ranking of anomalies above normal samples across nearly all thresholds.

AUC-PR captures the relationship between precision and recall over different anomaly-score thresholds and focuses exclusively on the positive (anomalous) class. By emphasizing precision at varying recall levels, AUC-PR is particularly informative for highly imbalanced settings—such as anomaly detection—where false positives are costly and anomalies constitute only a small portion of the data. A higher AUC-PR indicates that, across a broad range of operating points, the method retrieves a larger proportion of true anomalous samples while maintaining fewer false alarms, making it a sensitive measure of effectiveness under severe imbalance. Higher AUC-PR implies better retrieval of rare anomalous samples while maintaining lower false-alert rates.

Precision@K (also referred to as precision@rank-n) is defined as the proportion of ground-truth anomalies among the top-K test samples ranked by anomaly score. Specifically, test samples are sorted in descending order of their anomaly scores, the top-K samples are selected, and Precision@K is computed as the number of true anomalies within this set divided by \(\text{K}\). In the reported experiments, \(\text{K}\) is set to the number of ground-truth anomalies in the test split, i.e., \(\text{K}={\sum }_{\text{i}}1\left[{\text{y}}_{\text{i}}=1\right]\), which is consistent with the default behavior of PyOD’s precision_n_scores (Chen et al. 2024) when the parameter \(\text{n}\) is not explicitly specified. Importantly, this choice serves only to define the evaluation cutoff and is not used for detector training, threshold selection, or hyperparameter tuning. Precision@K is especially relevant in practical scenarios where analysts can only inspect a limited number of high-risk cases. Improvements in Precision@K indicate that the highest-ranked alerts contain more true anomalies, demonstrating stronger top-rank prioritization and greater operational utility. Higher Precision@K reflects stronger prioritization of truly anomalous samples in the top-ranked positions, which directly reduces manual inspection cost.

Following the evaluation protocols of anomaly detection (Ye et al. 2025), 50% of the normal samples are used for training and the remaining 50% is set aside for testing in each experiment. Performance is evaluated by taking the average result of 10 independent trials (with seed from 0 to 9) (Xu et al. 2023a). To characterize performance stability, the experiments adopt the standard deviations of AUC-ROC, AUC-PR, and Precision@K across datasets as stability measures. While AUC-ROC, AUC-PR and Precision@K evaluate a detector’s discriminative ability on individual datasets, they do not capture cross-dataset consistency. The mean rank metric addresses this limitation by ranking all competing methods on each dataset separately for AUC-ROC, AUC-PR, and Precision@K, and then averaging these ranks across datasets. This yields a dataset-invariant and scale-free indicator of relative overall performance, providing a robust and fair assessment of a model’s competitiveness and stability across heterogeneous benchmarks. Smaller mean rank values correspond to better overall performance, implying that the method consistently achieves higher scores of the three metrics across benchmarks.

Competing methods

HF-DIF is compared with 12 state-of-the-art unsupervised anomaly detection methods. The classical baseline methods include Isolation Forest (iForest) (Liu et al. 2012), Copula-based Outlier Detection (COPOD) (Li et al. 2020), Lightweight on-line detector of anomalies (LODA) (Pevný 2016), Histogram-based outlier score (HBOS) (Goldstein and Dengel 2012), Local Outlier Detection Methods via Graph Neural Networks (LUNAR) (Goodge et al. 2022) and Empirical Cumulative Distribution Function-based Outlier Detector (ECOD) (Li et al. 2023). The deep learning–based baseline models comprise Deep Noise Evaluation method (DNE) (Dai et al. 2025), Deep Isolation Forest (DIF) (Xu et al. 2023a), Scale Learning-based deep Anomaly Detection method (SLAD) (Xu et al. 2023b), Diffusion Time Estimation (DTE) (Livernoche et al. 2024), Disentangling-based Anomaly Detection (Disent-AD) (Ye et al. 2025), and Diffusion-Scheduled Denoising Autoencoder (DDAE) (Sattarov et al. 2025). These benchmark methods are summarized below.

iForest (Liu et al. 2012) introduces a fundamentally different approach by isolating anomalies rather than profiling normal points. Isolation enables the method to leverage sub-sampling extensively, making the algorithm highly scalable with linear time complexity, low memory requirements, and favorable performance on large datasets.

COPOD (Li et al. 2020) models multivariate data distributions using copulas to estimate tail probabilities for each data point, which indicates its level of extremeness or anomaly.

ECOD (Li et al. 2023) estimates the empirical cumulative distribution per dimension of the data and computes tail probabilities. Outlier scores are then calculated by aggregating these probabilities across dimensions.

DNE (Dai et al. 2025) is an unsupervised anomaly detection method for tabular data based on noise evaluation. By injecting noise with varying variance into normal samples and training a deep neural network to distinguish between noisy and clean patterns, DNE learns an effective decision boundary for identifying anomalies.

DIF (Xu et al. 2023a) is a representation scheme leveraging casually initialized neural networks to map raw data into random ensemble representations. Subsequently, random axis-parallel cuts are applied within these representations to partition the data. The synergy between random representations (subspaces) and partition-based isolation gives a more efficient separation of normal instances from anomalous ones.

SLAD (Xu et al. 2023b) considers the relationship between the dimensionality of the original sub-vectors and their transformed representations. SLAD learns to align the scale distribution, enabling it to model inherent regularities in the data.

DTE (Livernoche et al. 2024) addresses anomaly detection by estimating the distribution of diffusion times for a given input, utilizing either the mode or mean of this distribution as the anomaly score. In experiments, DTE-C (Livernoche et al. 2024), an efficient version of DTE, is employed.

Disent-AD (Ye et al. 2025) disentangles tabular features into two non-overlapping yet correlated subsets via a two-head self-attention mechanism to capture intrinsic inter-attribute correlations of normal data. Anomalies are identified through high reconstruction errors when either subset fails to reproduce the original input using the shared decoder.

DDAE (Sattarov et al. 2025) introduces a diffusion-based noise scheduling process into an autoencoder framework, training it to reconstruct clean inputs from progressively noised versions through timestep-conditioned denoising.

LODA (Pevný 2016) is a lightweight ensemble of one-dimensional histograms constructed on sparse random projections, which approximates the data density in the projected space and assigns anomaly scores via the average negative log-likelihood across these projections.

HBOS (Goldstein and Dengel 2012) builds independent univariate histograms for each feature, using fixed or adaptive bin widths to estimate normalized densities; outlier scores are obtained by combining the inverse bin heights across dimensions.

LUNAR (Goodge et al. 2022) unifies classical local outlier methods within a graph neural network message-passing framework on a k-nearest neighbor graph and learns an anomaly scoring function via a neural aggregator over neighbor distances combined with negative sampling.

Statistical significance and multiple-comparison correction

To assess whether the performance differences between HF-DIF and the baselines are statistically significant, two-sided paired t-tests are conducted for each evaluation metric, in accordance with standard practice in applied statistics [70, Sec. 10.4]. For each metric (i.e., AUC-ROC, AUC-PR, and Precision@K), the average performance score (over 10 independent trials) is recorded as a single scalar value for HF-DIF and each baseline on every dataset. Subsequently, for a given baseline, the differences between the HF-DIF scores and the baseline scores across all datasets are arranged into a difference vector, and a paired t-test is applied under the null hypothesis that the mean of this difference vector is zero. This procedure yields a \(\text{p}\)-value for each “HF-DIF vs. baseline” pair per metric.

In comparative evaluations of unsupervised anomaly detection methods, reporting mean scores alone is incomplete unless the observed differences are accompanied by a rigorous assessment of statistical significance. This requirement is especially important when a large number of detectors are compared on the same benchmark suite, because interpreting many pairwise tests without correction can markedly increase the risk of false positive conclusions. Since multiple pairwise comparisons are performed simultaneously, the family-wise error rate is controlled at a global significance level of a=0.05 via the Holm–Bonferroni correction (Holm 1979). Specifically, for each metric, the \(\text{p}\)-values of all "HF-DIF vs. baseline" comparisons are first sorted in ascending order, denoted as \(\text{p}\left(1\right)\le \text{p}\left(2\right)\le \dots \le \text{p}\left(\text{B}\right)\), where \(\text{B}\) is the number of baselines (\(\text{B}=12\)). Starting from the smallest \(\text{p}\left(1\right)\), \(\text{p}\left(\text{b}\right)\) is sequentially compared with the threshold a/(B-b+1). If p(b) is less than or equal to this threshold, the corresponding null hypothesis is rejected and the next p(b+1 is considered; once a p(b) exceeds its threshold, i.e., p(b)>a/(B-b+1), no further hypotheses are rejected. This step-down procedure of Holm–Bonferroni correction guarantees that, for each fixed metric, the probability of making at least one type-I error among all comparisons between HF-DIF and the baselines does not exceed 0.05.

Data Splitting, parameter settings and implementations

In accordance with the experimental protocol of DDAE (Sattarov et al. 2025), all datasets are standardized to zero mean and unit variance before training. In the unsupervised setting, each model under comparison is trained with bootstrapping on the entire dataset, and anomaly scores are evaluated for all samples within the same dataset. For a fair comparison, HF-DIF adopts the same parameter setting as DIF that uses 50 representations (\(\Gamma =50,\text{ d}=40\)) and 6 isolation trees per representation (\(\text{K}=6\)), with 256 as subsampling size (\(\uppsi =256\)) for each iTree; both HF-DIF and DIF process the tabular dataset depending upon fully-connected multi-layer-perceptron networks. The shrink coefficients \({\upeta }_{\text{r}},\text{r}=1,\dots ,\Gamma\), of HF-DIF is selected by the Representation-Adaptive Shrinkage strategy (see Section 5.1). For the DNE method (Dai et al. 2025), the network architecture is chosen as a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), which is a plain ReLU-activated feed-forward neural network consisting of four fully connected layers (size of each hidden layer is set to 256); the network is trained using AMSGrad optimizer with the learning rate \({10}^{-4}\) and the weight decay \(5\times {10}^{-4}\); Gaussian noise is adopted in the noise generation, maximum noise level \({\upsigma }_{\text{max}}=2\), and the number of different noise distributions is set as 3. For SLAD, following the parameter configurations in Xu et al. 2023b, the representation dimensionality \(\text{h}\) is set as 128, the magnification factor \(\upgamma\) is set as 200, the number of randomly sampled sub-vectors \(\text{c}\) and label number \(\text{r}\) are set as 10 and 20, respectively. According to the recommendation in Sattarov et al. (2025), DTE-C employs a three-hidden-layer MLP with size [256, 512, 256], the maximum bin number is set as 400, the learning rate and weight decay are set as 0.0001 and 0.0005, respectively. For Disent-AD, following the hyperparameter setting in Ye et al. (2025), the learning rate is set as \({10}^{-4}\), training epoch is 200, batch size is selected from the set of \(\left\{{2}^{6},{2}^{7},...,{2}^{11}\right\}\) according to training sample amount, and the channel number of latent features is set as 512. Following the parameter configurations in Sattarov et al. (2025), DDAE adopts a feedforward encoder-decoder with LeakyReLU activations, trained by Adam and Glorot initialization for up to 100 epochs; its network width is prioritized over depth (2–3 layers, \(\ge 512\) units per layer); the diffusion steps is selected from \(\left[\text{500,1000}\right]\); timestep-embedding dimensions varies from 4 to 32. Both LODA and HBOS can automatically select their hyper-parameter nbins according to Birgé-Rozenholc criterium (Birgé and Rozenholc 2006). For Lunar, the neaerest neighborhood parameter \(\text{k}\) is selected in the range \(\left[\text{5,10,15,20,25,30}\right]\).

All the anomaly detection methods in our experiments are implemented in Python. Specifically, iForest, COPOD, LUNAR, LODA, HBOS and ECOD are implemented based on the open-source library PyOD (Chen et al. 2024). The source code of DIF is released by the library DeepOD (Xu et al. 2023a). The code implementation of the proposed HF-DIF is also based on the library DeepOD. The source code of SLAD is taken from its original authors (Xu et al. 2023b). DTE-C is run based on the DTE package (Sattarov et al. 2025). DNE (Dai et al. 2025) is implemented based on Pytorch and DeepOD. Disent-AD is implemented using the Disent-AD package (Ye et al. 2025). The source code of DDAE is derived from the AnoDDAE package (Sattarov et al. 2025). The experiments are conducted under the environment of a workstation with Intel Xeon Silver 6133 CPU, a single NVIDIA RTX4090 GPU, and 64GB RAM.

It should be noted that in the experimental implementations related to HF-DIF, the structural properties of the trained Isolation Forest are explicitly utilized. Specifically, the underlying tree structures preserve the topological metadata, enabling direct access to the specific splitting feature index and the exact threshold value of every internal node. Furthermore, for any given input dataset, the deterministic traversal trajectory of each sample—from the root node to its corresponding leaf node—is extracted as a sparse indicator matrix via the model's decision path function. Therefore, leveraging the iForest's topological metadata enables fast, resource-efficient derivation of Feature Importance Scores (refer to Section 4.3).

AUC-ROC is a widely used evaluation metric for anomaly detection methods; however, in heavily imbalanced settings where normal samples constitute the dominant majority, this metric tends to yield overly optimistic assessments of performance. AUC-ROC incorporates True Negatives in its calculation, potentially masking poor performance on the minority class. In contrast, AUC-PR captures the relationship between precision and recall over different anomaly-score thresholds and focuses exclusively on the positive (anomalous) class. By emphasizing precision at varying recall levels, AUC-PR is particularly informative in highly imbalanced settings where false positives are costly and anomalies are rare. A higher AUC-PR implies that the detector maintains higher precision at increasing recall levels, i.e., fewer false alarms while detecting a larger fraction of true anomalies.

Unlike AUC-ROC, which provides a threshold-independent assessment of global separability by aggregating performance across all possible operating points, Precision@K is explicitly budgeted and top-heavy: it focuses on the quality of the highest-confidence alerts that would be inspected in practice. Precision@K is also more localized than AUC-PR. While AUC-PR summarizes the precision–recall trade-off over a continuum of score thresholds and is particularly informative under severe class imbalance, Precision@K corresponds to a single cutoff on the ranked list and directly quantifies the expected "hit rate" under a fixed inspection capacity. Consequently, Precision@K complements AUC-ROC and AUC-PR by emphasizing operational utility in the extreme top ranks, where false alarms translate most directly into manual review cost.

DIF applies deep random projections to map the data into multiple projected subspaces, thereby increasing representation diversity. DIF’s consistent improvements over iForest across different metrics provide empirical support for the benefit of projection-enhanced representation diversity in unsupervised anomaly detection.

Mechanistically, HF-DIF converts DIF’s projection-induced diversity into useful separability through the coupled use of the iForest-based Feature Evaluator (iFFE) with adaptive dimension selection capability and the Hierarchical Feature Selection scheme. This two-stage design distills the enlarged pool of projected features into a separability-relevant feature subset: iFFE prioritizes projected features that improve isolation-based anomaly–normal discrimination, while hierarchical feature-selection consolidates those features whose utility remains consistently beneficial across subspaces and filters out unstable candidates. HF-DIF’s improvement over DIF is experimentally validated as a robust, cross-dataset advantage under the same benchmarking protocol and remains supported after Holm–Bonferroni FWER control. In the pairwise comparison tables, HF-DIF consistently outperforms DIF, and the superiority is not confined to a single measurement perspective; it persists across multiple metrics, i.e., AUC-ROC, AUC-PR, and Precision@K, indicating that the gain reflects a stable mean improvement across datasets rather than a metric-specific fluctuation. This validation is directly aligned with the intended design effect: by screening out low-utility projections and consolidating consistently beneficial ones, HF-DIF prevents projection diversity from diluting anomaly–normal separation and instead turns it into a reliable source of discriminative structure. Consequently, HF-DIF should be interpreted as a separability-oriented projection ensemble rather than a detector that benefits from projection diversity alone. By transforming "more projections" into "more useful projections" via iFFE-driven utility scoring and hierarchical cross-subspace consolidation, HF-DIF yields a systematic improvement in ranking quality and anomaly prioritization that generalizes across heterogeneous datasets.

Among the 12 baselines (compared with HF-DIF), DNE, Disent-AD, DTE-C, and DIF obtain the lowest cross-metric mean ranks—computed by ranking methods on each dataset under each metric and then averaging the resulting rank positions across the three evaluation metrics; accordingly, they constitute the strongest baseline group and exhibit consistently strong performance across all metrics.

DNE (Deep Noise Evaluation) is the closest competitor to HF-DIF—it leads the baseline group under AUC-ROC and stays competitive under AUC-PR and Precision@K—yet it remains second only to HF-DIF overall in the benchmark. Its main limitation is the inherent coupling to corruption design—performance varies with noise scale and noise family—so the learned noise magnitude response can be an imperfect proxy for subtle or subspace-structured anomalies in heterogeneous tabular benchmarks. HF-DIF addresses this limitation via a different route: rather than shaping the scoring function through a corruption-based surrogate, it couples DIF-style projections with iFFE and Hierarchical Feature Selection to identify and retain only separation-beneficial projected features, thereby preventing weak projections from diluting anomaly–normal separation and yielding a more consistent benchmark-wide advantage over DNE and the remaining baselines.

Disent-AD models normal cross-feature dependencies by splitting correlated attributes into two CorrSets (via two-head self-attention) and scoring each sample by the sum of the two reconstruction errors. This formulation makes dependency preservation an explicit learning target, which is well suited to anomalies that manifest as violations of normal inter-feature relationships, even when marginal single-feature deviations are weak. However, the effective diversity available at test time is largely confined to these two CorrSet views, and the cosine/orthogonality disentangling term serves as a training-time constraint while the inference-time anomaly score remains purely reconstruction-based. Consequently, when anomalies are not well characterized as correlation breakage (e.g., subspace-local or interaction-driven deviations) or when weakly informative views dominate reconstruction, Disent-AD lacks a mechanism to screen out low-utility factors and reliably sharpen anomaly–normal separation. In comparison, HF-DIF is less constrained by a fixed small set of reconstruction views: it can draw from many projected subspaces and then prioritize projected features through iFFE and consolidate them via Hierarchical Feature Selection, enabling diversity to contribute only when it consistently improves separability—consistent with HF-DIF’s stronger and more stable benchmark performance than Disent-AD in the reported tables.

DTE-C leverages diffusion-time estimation to derive a manifold-aware yet efficient anomaly score by learning a parametric predictor of the diffusion-time statistic, and its categorical formulation is designed to be less sensitive to the choice of the diffusion horizon. However, inference reduces to a single diffusion-time summary statistic, which can smooth away fine-grained deviations and limits the method’s ability to selectively exploit heterogeneous feature cues when anomalies are subspace-local or interaction-driven rather than well captured by a global manifold-distance proxy. By contrast, HF-DIF uses DIF's projected features as candidates and relies on iFFE plus Hierarchical Feature Selection to keep only those projections that exhibit stable, separation-relevant utility, thereby preventing weak projections from being absorbed into the final scoring rule and accounting for HF-DIF’s more consistent advantage over DTE-C across heterogeneous benchmarks.

Across Tables 2, 3 and 4, SLAD, HBOS, and LODA show less competitive aggregate profiles than the leading baselines. SLAD's main weakness is an objective mismatch: it constructs training samples from randomly sampled index subspaces of the original feature set and optimizes scale learning via distribution alignment, while stating that this scale-learning objective is not directly related to anomaly detection; consequently, the optimization can emphasize subspace-level alignment without reliably sharpening anomaly–normal separability, especially in high dimensions where correlation-aware weighting is avoided and the method defaults to uniform feature weights due to bias/overhead concerns. HBOS achieves efficiency by estimating per-feature marginal densities with histograms and aggregating them into an outlier score under a feature-independence assumption; because this scoring is driven by global marginal rarity rather than neighborhood context (or cross-feature interactions), HBOS is well suited to global deviations but can be less sensitive to local outliers. LODA injects randomness via sparse random projections, but each component detector is still a one-dimensional histogram and the aggregated score corresponds to a product-form likelihood only under a strong projection-independence assumption; correlated projections and histogram smoothing can therefore dilute the contrast contributed by informative directions. Notably, this also clarifies why DIF can outperform LODA even without explicit selection: although both use random projections, LODA's weak learners are 1D density estimates with an independence-leaning aggregation, whereas DIF's isolation-style scoring in projected spaces is not tied to histogram likelihood factorization and can yield stronger separation from the same projection diversity.

6.3 Ablation study

Both DIF and HF-DIF generate deep random projected representations as an initial step, which constitutes an important advantage in capturing feature diversity across different subspaces. To assess the effect of feature selection under this representation mechanism, deep random projected representations are combined with eight state-of-the-art unsupervised feature selection methods. The eight unsupervised feature selection methods adopted by DIF are summarized as follows.

Laplacian Score (LapScore) (He et al. 2005) ranks features by their locality-preserving strength on a KNN graph, capturing the intrinsic local geometry of the data manifold. It provides an efficient, learner-agnostic filter that enables unsupervised feature ranking without coupling to any specific downstream model.

Spectral Feature Selection (SPEC) (Zhao and Liu 2007) ranks features by how consistently each feature aligns with the structure of a similarity graph, offering a unified spectral view of unsupervised (data-defined similarity) feature selection.

Multi-Cluster Feature Selection (MCFS) (Cai et al. 2010) combines Laplacian-eigenmap embedding on a KNN graph with sparse regression (L1-regularized least squares) to score and rank features by their contributions to the embedding, thereby selecting a compact subset that preserves multi-cluster geometry and supports efficient clustering.

Unsupervised Discriminative Feature Selection (UDFS) (Yang et al. 2011) performs unsupervised batch feature selection by jointly modeling local discriminative structure and manifold neighborhoods, using \({\text{l}}_{\text{2,1}}\) regularization to induce row-sparsity so that feature importance is given by the row norms of the learned projection.

High contrast subspaces (HiCS) (Keller et al. 2012) selects high-contrast subspaces for density-based outlier ranking. It quantifies contrast via conditional dependence, repeatedly testing whether a feature's marginal distribution shifts under subspace-based conditioning. The tests are instantiated with Welch's t-test and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test.

Feature Bagging (FB) (Lazarevic and Kumar 2005) addresses the curse of dimensionality by aggregating scores from detectors on random feature subsets, enabling the robust identification of outliers discernible only in lower-dimensional subspaces.

Outlier detection ensemble framework with embedded feature selection (ODEFS) (Cheng et al. 2020) integrates feature selection and outlier detection into a unified ensemble framework via pairwise ranking and thresholded self-paced learning. This embedded approach simultaneously optimizes feature relevance and training reliability, ensuring superior noise resilience and detection accuracy with linear scalability.

Robust principal component analysis (RobustPCA) (Candès et al. 2011) overcomes the fragility of classical PCA to gross corruptions by employing Principal Component Pursuit (PCP) to decompose the data matrix into a low-rank structure representing normal patterns and a sparse component that effectively isolates anomalies.

For a fair comparison, all these feature selection methods follow the same non-hierarchical (or hierarchical) selection scheme. Under the non-hierarchical scheme, feature selection is performed within each projected subspace (the same as DIF’s random projected representation) with a pre-specified ratio (e.g., 5%), and the retained features are then concatenated to form the final feature representation. By contrast, under the hierarchical feature-selection scheme, candidate features are first selected within each projected subspace (representation) and then globally re-evaluated to form a compact feature subset (consistent with HF-DIF’s hierarchical scheme). In the first-level feature selection, the top 25% of features are extracted from each projected subspace. In the second-level feature selection, the top 20% of features are then selected from the union of all first-level extracted features. Notably, the non-hierarchical and hierarchical schemes are configured to yield the same overall feature selection ratio (\(5\text{\%}=25\text{\%}\times 20\text{\%}\)), thereby ensuring a fair and controlled comparison.

Beyond the average-row summaries (mentioned above), the dataset-level comparisons in Tables 9, 10 and 11 further show that general-purpose UFS methods often fail to improve anomaly detection when used as a plug-in feature selector under both non-hierarchical and hierarchical feature-selection schemes. For example, on AUC-ROC in Table 9 under the hierarchical scheme, LapScore, MCFS, and UDFS underperform DIF on 19 out of 20 datasets (SPEC: 18 out of 20). Under the hierarchical scheme, this tendency is particularly clear for representative generic methods such as LapScore, MCFS, and UDFS: they fall below DIF (a reference detector without feature selection) on the majority of datasets for AUC-ROC (Table 9), and the same behavior persists on the imbalance-sensitive metrics AUC-PR (Table 10) and Precision@K (Table 11). In contrast, detection-oriented methods exhibit much stronger dataset-wise robustness under the same setting. Specifically, ODEFS, FB, and iFFE do not fall below DIF on any dataset across Tables 9, 10 and 11 under the hierarchical scheme, while HiCS and RobustPCA fall below DIF only on the Fraud dataset in terms of AUC-PR and remain above DIF elsewhere. This systematic difference is consistent with the methodological intent: HiCS, FB, ODEFS, RobustPCA, and iFFE are more directly aligned with anomaly detection (favoring anomalous–normal separability), whereas LapScore, SPEC, MCFS, and UDFS aim to preserve generic manifold/cluster structure or global information content, which can dilute sparse anomaly cues and consequently degrade ranking quality.

The hierarchical feature selection scheme provides a broadly applicable benefit over the non-hierarchical scheme. Across Tables 9, 10 and 11, the hierarchical scheme yields equal or higher performance than the non-hierarchical scheme for all nine UFS baselines on every dataset, indicating a consistent improvement tendency. The benefit is particularly evident for iFFE: when moving from the non-hierarchical feature-selection scheme to the hierarchical feature-selection scheme, iFFE improves AUC-ROC, AUC-PR, and Precision@K across all 20 datasets, and its average-row gains are + 1.8 AUC-ROC (80.1 vs. 78.3 in Table 9), + 1.4 AUC-PR (32.8 vs. 31.4 in Table 10), and + 1.5 Precision@K (38.7 vs. 37.2 in Table 11). This trend is consistent with the fact that deep random projections yield heterogeneous projected subspaces whose contribution to anomalous–normal separability varies substantially; hierarchical feature-selection scheme introduces cross-subspace refinement so that candidate features originating from subspaces with stronger anomalous–normal separability are preferentially retained, while features from less suitable subspaces are suppressed. By contrast, the non-hierarchical scheme performs feature selection independently within each subspace and lacks a mechanism to re-rank and allocate selection preference across subspaces, which can be suboptimal when subspace quality is uneven.

Finally, even when iFFE is evaluated under the hierarchical feature-selection scheme (its best-performing setting), it still underperforms HF-DIF across all three metrics. In the average-row summaries (averaged over datasets), HF-DIF exceeds iFFE by + 2.5 AUC-ROC points (82.6 vs. 80.1 in Table 9), + 2.1 AUC-PR points (34.9 vs. 32.8 in Table 10), and + 0.5 Precision@K points (39.2 vs. 38.7 in Table 11). For AUC-ROC (Table 9), HF-DIF outperforms iFFE on 19 out of 20 datasets, and the two methods achieve the same AUC-ROC score on the Fraud dataset. For AUC-PR (Table 10), HF-DIF outperforms iFFE on 19 out of 20 datasets, except on the Optdigits dataset, where the two methods obtain the same AUC-PR score; for Precision@K (Table 11), HF-DIF outperforms iFFE on all 20 datasets. This advantage of HF-DIF over iFFE is consistent with HF-DIF’s adaptive shrinkage strategy: instead of setting a fixed selection ratio uniformly across all projected subspaces, HF-DIF adaptively determines how many features to retain from each subspace according to its estimated contribution to anomalous–normal separability, thereby selecting features that contribute most to this separability.

A useful way to characterize this time–cost landscape is to decompose, for each method and dataset, the total training time into (i) feature selection time and (ii) the remaining training time (total minus selection). This decomposition separates selection-induced time cost from the downstream detector-training component, and clarifies whether a method’s total-time increase is mainly selection-driven or also changes the subsequent training workload. Under this view, iFFE exhibits a selection-dominant cost profile: averaged over the 20 datasets, iFFE spends 11.3s on feature selection and 115.9s in total, so its remaining component is 104.6s, essentially identical to DIF’s mean total training time (104.5s). In other words, iFFE’s average total-time increase relative to DIF is largely attributable to the added selection stage, rather than a systematic increase in downstream training time.

In terms of feature selection time (Table 12), iFFE is not the most time-consuming UFS baseline. Averaged over the 20 datasets, iFFE spends 11.3s on feature selection and is the 4th fastest among the nine UFS baselines (from fastest to slowest: LapScore 2.0s, RobustPCA 4.9s, HiCS 6.2s, iFFE 11.3s, FB 16.0s, ODEFS 17.1s, SPEC 48.4s, MCFS 98.9s, UDFS 624.3s). Thus, relative to the fastest UFS baselines, iFFE incurs only a modest small additional feature selection time on average (e.g., + 5.1s vs. HiCS, + 6.4s vs. RobustPCA, and + 9.3s vs. LapScore). Median-based summaries reinforce the same conclusion. iFFE achieves median and mean feature selection times of 4.4s and 11.3s, respectively—comparable to RobustPCA (4.3s and 4.9s) but substantially lower than UDFS (66.6s and 624.3s). Overall, aside from the LapScore comparison at the unadjusted level, iFFE’s feature-selection-time differences from the faster baselines are not statistically distinguishable under error-controlled testing, suggesting that iFFE does not impose a substantial additional complexity burden in feature selection time.

As illustrated in Table 15, HF-DIF exhibits shorter inference times than DIF across all 20 benchmark datasets. Quantitatively, DIF requires an average inference time of 16.77 s and a median of 1.65 s. In comparison, HF-DIF reduces these metrics to an average of 0.66 s and a median of 0.09 s. In terms of relative speedup (defined as the inference time of DIF divided by that of HF-DIF), HF-DIF achieves a median speedup of 16.69 × and a mean speedup of 59.09 × over DIF (the mean is skewed by an extreme 873 × outlier on the Fraud dataset). The higher inference latency in DIF is primarily attributed to its representation ensemble mechanism. Specifically, DIF generates multiple diverse feature spaces through deep random projections and constructs a corresponding iForest for each space to perform anomaly scoring. This architecture inevitably leads to a massive overall model scale, which severely constrains inference speed. HF-DIF addresses this bottleneck through an anomaly-detection-oriented hierarchical feature selection. This strategy not only reduces the dimensionality of the projected features but also improves the anomalous-normal separability. Consequently, the final iForest in the second level of HF-DIF is highly compact. It requires fewer iTrees and operates with shallower maximum depths than the ensemble of iForests in DIF, thereby accelerating the inference process while maintaining detection performance. The proposed HF-DIF focuses on unsupervised isolation-based anomaly detection and builds upon the standard Deep Isolation Forest (DIF) framework by introducing the hierarchical feature-selection mechanism. In this context, the assumption of uniformly random split thresholds is not meant to model the data distribution itself, but rather to reflect the algorithmic design of isolation forests: node split thresholds are typically sampled uniformly at random within the range of the selected feature. This is the canonical setting adopted in the original iForest work and in many subsequent isolation-based methods, where the ensemble of a large number of iTrees is used to average out the bias of any individual random split. Many variants of iForest employ some innovations in split-threshold selection, such as clustering based threshold selection, kernel density estimation. However, these methods have to suffer from heavy computational burden compared to uniform random selection. This efficiency can be attributed to the way in which iFFE evaluates feature relevance. Rather than performing computationally intensive optimization over the full set of projected features, iFFE derives separability-relevant statistics from the internal parameters of trained iForests built on diverse projected representations. Because each iForest tree is trained on a small random subsample and each split is generated without node-wise optimization, the resulting model remains lightweight in both computation and storage. Consequently, iFFE can compute its subsequent parameter-based statistics from a compact summary of the projected representations rather than from data-sized feature sets.

Another important contrast concerns HF-DIF versus iFFE in feature selection time (Table 12). HF-DIF's mean feature selection time is 15.6s (median 5.9s), exceeding iFFE (11.3s, median 4.4s). This is because HF-DIF employs a representation-adaptive shrinkage mechanism that adaptively tunes subspace-specific shrink coefficients to determine how many features to retain in each projected subspace, whereas iFFE follows a fixed-ratio configuration without subspace-wise adaptive determination. The gap is visible on large datasets—for example, Donors (selection time 119.8s for HF-DIF vs. 87.1s for iFFE), Fraud (42.8s vs. 29.1s), and Backdoor (38.1s vs. 27.0s).

Moreover, an unadjusted two-sided paired \(\text{t}\)-test is performed to compare DIF and HF-DIF in terms of training time (as illustrated in Table 12), under the null hypothesis that the mean per-dataset training time difference equals zero. Over the 20 datasets with paired measurements, HF-DIF shows a higher mean training time than DIF (111.16 s vs. 104.48 s in Table 12), with a mean paired difference (HF-DIF − DIF) of 6.67 s, which attributes to the additional computations introduced by the iFFE module and the hierarchical feature-selection scheme. However, the unadjusted test yields \({\text{p}}_{raw}=0.057\) (as shown in Table 13), which does not meet the conventional significance criterion at a=0.05. Therefore, under the unadjusted paired \(\text{t}\)-test, HF-DIF does not exhibit a statistically significant increase in training time compared with DIF.

Although HF-DIF incurs a higher feature selection time than iFFE evaluated under the hierarchical selection scheme, HF-DIF consistently achieves better detection performance, attaining higher AUC-ROC, AUC-PR, and Precision@K in Tables 9, 10, 11. This motivates an explicit cost–benefit assessment of whether the additional time introduced by feature selection is justified by the performance gain.

To evaluate the cost–benefit of incorporating feature selection, two relative-change measures are defined to jointly quantify the training-time overhead and the corresponding performance gain. Let the number of datasets be \(\aleph\)   (in this ablation study, \(\aleph\) ). Given an unsupervised feature-selection (UFS) method ℏ (under the hierarchical selection scheme) and a dataset Un, \(\aleph\) the relative training-time change of ℏ with respect to DIF is defined as

$${\Delta T}_{DIF}\left(\hslash\right)=\frac1{\aleph} \sum\limits_{n=1}^\aleph\frac{T\left(\hslash,U_n\right)-T\left(DIF,U_n\right)}{T\left(DIF,U_n\right)}$$

where \(\left(\hslash,U_n\right)\) denote the total training time of method ℏ on dataset \({\text{U}}_{\text{n}}\).

The relative performance change of ℏ with respect to DIF is defined as

$${\Delta S}_{DIF}\left(\hslash\right)=\frac1{\aleph}\sum_{n=1}^\aleph\frac{S\left(\hslash,U_n\right)-S\left(DIF,U_n\right)}{S\left(DIF,U_n\right)}$$

where S(ℏ,Un)is the detection performance method ℏ on dataset \({\text{U}}_{\text{n}}\), S(ℏ,Un) is instantiated by AUC-ROC, AUC-PR, or Precision@K. ∆SDIF(ℏ) is not guaranteed to be positive. Tables 9, 10, 11 show that several general-purpose feature selection baselines (e.g., LapScore, SPEC, MCFS, and UDFS) do not consistently improve anomaly detection performance, and they yield lower performance than DIF on multiple datasets under the evaluated metrics.

Using∆SDIF(ℏ)and ∆SDIF(ℏ) as the horizontal and vertical axes, respectively, Fig. 15(a)-(c) provide a compact visualization of the DIF-relative cost–benefit trade-off for different methods in terms of AUC-ROC, AUC-PR, and Precision@K. The first quadrant (∆SDIF(ℏ)>0 and ∆SDIF(ℏ)>0) corresponds to the methods that improve detection performance over DIF at the expense of additional training time, where points closer to the upper-left indicate more favorable trade-offs. As shown in Fig. 15(a)-(c), HF-DIF and iFFE (under the hierarchical selection scheme) consistently occupy the most favorable positions among the competing methods. In contrast, LapScore, SPEC, MCFS, and UDFS fall into the fourth quadrant in Fig. 15(a)-(c), indicating that they incur additional training cost without delivering performance improvements over DIF, and are therefore less suitable for unsupervised anomaly detection under the experimental conditions considered in this study. Although HiCS, RobustPCA, and Feature Bagging (FB) incur relatively small time overhead, their performance gains are marginal, making their overall trade-off still less attractive than that of HF-DIF and iFFE.

Table 16 illustrates the storage consumption of DIF and HF-DIF. For HF-DIF, the iFFE module and the hierarchical feature selection scheme lead to only limited additional memory overhead. In particular, iFFE performs feature selection by reusing the structural parameters of trained iForests to derive feature-evaluation scores, rather than constructing additional feature representations (e.g., extra feature matrices or auxiliary embeddings). This design choice is memory-efficient because an iForest stores only compact tree-structure parameters (e.g., split-feature indices and node-level statistics) for a fixed number of trees and nodes, and it does not retain data-sized, per-sample representations; hence, the model storage scales primarily with the forest structure rather than with the dataset size. Moreover, from the perspective of feature representation, the hierarchical scheme inherits DIF’s sparse-storage advantage by avoiding dense, full-dimensional intermediate representations and instead maintaining only a compact record of the selected feature subset. Specifically, it retains only the projection parameters associated with the selected features together with the corresponding feature-index list. Consequently, as illustrated in Table 16, HF-DIF introduces only a modest increase in memory usage and does not create a heavy memory burden.

7 Conclusion

This paper proposes the hierarchical feature-selected deep isolation forest (HF-DIF) for unsupervised anomaly detection. HF-DIF introduces an iForest-based hierarchical feature-selection scheme that cooperates with deep nonlinear random projections of input data, enabling unsupervised anomaly detection to operate on distinguishable and informative features extracted from diverse projected representations. The hierarchical feature selection scheme of HF-DIF effectively leverages the intrinsic structure and parameters of the constructed iForests. It explores distinguishable features advantageous to unsupervised anomaly detection from both local and global perspectives while preserving computational efficiency. Theoretical analysis confirms HF-DIF’s superior capability to separate anomalous and normal samples compared with DIF, thereby establishing the structural advantage of feature-guided diversity over purely deep random projections. Extensive experiments further validate these theoretical findings, demonstrating HF-DIF’s consistent improvements in detection accuracy (e.g., AUC-ROC, AUC-PR, and Precision@k) and stability across a variety of benchmark tabular datasets, while retaining the scalability inherent to isolation-based methods.

In future work, HF-DIF can be extended to time-series scenarios and integrated with interpretable anomaly-detection modules to enhance its applicability across real-world settings. The main idea for extending HF-DIF to time-series scenarios is to design a dynamic deep projection model that projects and embeds time-series features, while adapting the iForest-based feature evaluator to an online setting. The key to an interpretable HF-DIF is to establish an explicit mapping between original semantic features and the selected projected features, thereby endowing the randomly generated, anomaly-sensitive projections with interpretability.
